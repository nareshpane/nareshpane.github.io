Introductory · 01

From a population to a monthly estimate

The LFS targets the civilian, non-institutionalized population aged 15 and over. In the provinces it excludes people on reserves and other Indigenous settlements, full-time Armed Forces members and institutional residents. National totals cover the ten provinces; the territories are published separately. The population represented by an estimate is thus a carefully defined survey population, not every resident of Canada.

Dwelling. A physical residential accommodation; the address frame selects dwellings.

Household. People sharing the selected dwelling as their usual residence. Its composition can change.

Respondent and observation. A respondent may answer for other household members. A population observation is an eligible person; a cluster is a primary sampling unit.

The 2025 design generally selects geographic clusters, then dwellings within them. Clusters contain approximately 100–400 dwellings; typically six clusters are selected within a stratum. Prince Edward Island uses a one-stage address-list sample. Labour force information is collected for all eligible civilian household members aged 15+. For people aged 70+, initial responses are carried forward through the remaining five interviews to reduce burden.

Settings do not establish coverage. The northern scene is an illustrative winter landscape. Provincial coverage excludes people on reserves and other Indigenous settlements, institutional residents and full-time Armed Forces members. Some very low-density remote dwellings are excluded from sampling, although the guide includes those remote populations in weighting control totals. A frame gap is distinct from a target-population exclusion, and a picture cannot quantify its bias. 2025 Guide §4.
Territories require a separate design story. Selected dwellings are interviewed every three months over two years, for eight interviews. Approximately one-eighth begins each quarter. Territorial estimates are three-month moving averages. Coverage is about 95% in Yukon and the Northwest Territories and about 93% in Nunavut; do not apply the provincial six-month rotation diagram to them.

Introductory · 02

Three statuses, three denominators

Employment takes priority. A person who worked even one hour for pay or profit in the reference week is employed; unpaid work in a family business also counts. A jobholder temporarily absent for qualifying reasons is employed. People without work are unemployed if they meet the official search, temporary-layoff or future-start conditions and the availability rules.

Availability includes some exceptions for illness/disability, personal or family responsibilities, vacation and a near-future job. A full-time student currently attending school and seeking full-time work is not considered available. “Not looking” alone is insufficient to classify someone outside the labour force: qualifying temporary layoffs and future job starts do not require active search.

\[\begin{gathered}L=E+U\\[5pt] u=\frac UL\\[5pt] e=\frac EP\\[5pt] p=\frac LP.\end{gathered}\]

Here \(P\) is the relevant survey population aged 15+, \(E\) employment, \(U\) unemployment and \(L\) the labour force. The unemployment rate conditions on participation; the employment and participation rates use the entire in-scope population. Rates displayed as percentages multiply these fractions by 100. At the population level, \(e=p(1-u)\).

Synthetic count calculator
A falling unemployment rate need not mean more jobs. With \(E=600,U=60\), unemployment is 9.09%. If 20 unemployed people stop searching and no one finds a job, \(U=40,L=640\), so unemployment falls to 6.25%. Employment remains 600 and participation falls from 66% to 64% when \(P=1,000\).

2025 Guide, §2 and §3 definitions: availability, temporary layoff, work.

Intermediate · 03

Probability sampling is the foundation of inference

Fix a finite population \(\mathcal U=\{1,\ldots,N\}\) and its values \(y_k\). The randomness in design-based inference is in the selected sample \(s\), not in a fictional assumption that the population has been generated independently. Each eligible unit needs a known, positive inclusion probability \(\pi_k=P_d(k\in s)\). Pairwise probabilities \(\pi_{kl}\) carry dependence between selections.

\[\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned}\pi_{ij}&=P_d(\text{cluster }i\text{ selected})\\&\quad\times P_d(\text{dwelling }j\text{ selected}\mid i)\end{aligned}\\[5pt] d_{ij}=\pi_{ij}^{-1}.\end{gathered}\]

This is the conditional probability identity for a dwelling reached through its cluster. It is not an independence assumption. If cluster inclusion is 0.2 and conditional dwelling inclusion is 0.1, overall inclusion is 0.02 and the base weight is 50. All eligible household members are reached through that dwelling; they are not independent person draws.

SRS benchmarkChoose n of N units without replacement, each sample equally likely. \(\pi_k=n/N\). Convenient theory, not the production LFS design.
StratificationPartition geography into strata, then sample within each. Known stratum totals allow representation and can lower variance.
ClusteringSample groups of nearby dwellings. Collection is cheaper; similar outcomes within groups can reduce information per interview.
PPS, systematic selection and unequal probabilities

Probability proportional to size (PPS) gives larger clusters a greater chance of selection, with a frame size measure such as dwelling count. Combining PPS cluster selection with fewer dwellings selected per larger cluster can yield approximately constant dwelling probabilities within strata. Across strata, allocation can still produce unequal weights. Inclusion probabilities in PPS without replacement depend on the exact algorithm; they are not always simply \(mM_i/\sum_iM_i\), particularly for certainty units.

Systematic selection chooses a random start and then every \(k\)th dwelling in an ordered list. Marginal probabilities may be uniform, but selections are dependent; periodic patterns in the frame can affect precision. The 2017 document describes PPS and systematic methods in detail. Those algorithmic details are labelled historical here rather than assumed unchanged in the 2025 redesign.

Design comparisons should hold the outcome, target, estimator and collection budget fixed. Stratification helps most when stratum means differ and within-stratum variation is smaller. Clustering need not increase variance for every variable; the usual loss depends on positive within-cluster correlation.

2017 Methodology, Chapter 2 (historical technical design); 2025 Guide, §4 (current design summary).

Survey design · from geography to probability

How Primary and Secondary Sampling Units Are Constructed and Selected

Build a frame first; randomly select from that frame second. A neighbourhood boundary is not itself a selected sample.

Geographic domains, strata, PSUs and SSUs

The 2025 redesign uses 2021 Census geography and characteristics, with census and administrative information to group similar clusters. It was phased in from April through September 2025. Regular strata seek homogeneity in key labour market variables; remote strata manage collection costs; large-cluster strata accommodate large apartment or condominium buildings; high-income strata support the needs of other household surveys. The guide says these four types are sampled at the same rate for the LFS; this does not mean every province or domain has the same sampling fraction.

Stratification controls representation and can reduce variance when within-stratum variation is small relative to between-stratum differences. Allocation must also meet geographic reliability targets, collection costs and response-burden constraints. A rotation panel specifies entry and exit timing. It is not a geographic stratum.

Constructing the geographic frame

In the current guide, clusters are contiguous geographic areas containing approximately 100–400 dwellings, and typically six clusters are selected per stratum. Dwellings in a selected cluster are extracted from Statistics Canada’s Statistical Building Register (SBgR). This current description should take precedence over older address-frame terminology.

Historical construction detail: 2017 §2.4 versus the 2025 guide

The 2017 publication describes a target of 200 households, using 2011 Census dissemination areas (DAs). Small contiguous DAs were joined; large DAs were split using dissemination blocks or block faces. Its general accepted range was 100–600 households, with some Toronto high-rise PSUs of 600–1,000 households grouped into special strata. Compactness reduced travel, but very small PSUs increased replacement costs and geographic spread.

The 2025 guide instead describes approximately 100–400 dwellings, new clusters and strata based on the 2021 Census, and the SBgR. It does not supply the detailed new merge/split algorithm. We therefore use the historical account to explain the construction problem, without claiming that its exact thresholds, exceptions or algorithm remain current.

Selecting PSUs: PPS and the historical RHC procedure

\[P_d(\text{PSU }i\text{ selected})=\pi_i.\]

A positive size measure \(M_i\), often related to dwelling or household counts, makes larger clusters more likely to be selected under probability-proportional-to-size (PPS) sampling. In a simplified with-replacement design, the per-draw probability is

\[\begin{gathered}p_i=\frac{M_i}{\sum_j M_j}\\[5pt] \pi_i=1-(1-p_i)^m\\[5pt]\text{for }m\text{ independent draws}.\end{gathered}\]

The second expression is the chance of being selected at least once in that specified teaching design. Neither \(p_i\) nor \(mp_i\) is automatically the final inclusion probability under the actual LFS. Without-replacement designs, certainty units, grouping, rounding and updating require their own probabilities.

Rao–Hartley–Cochran (RHC): random groups followed by PPS

In the general RHC construction, randomly partition a stratum’s PSUs into groups of prescribed counts, then select one PSU by PPS within each group. Conditional on a realized partition \(G\), let the group size total and conditional selection probability be

\[M_g=\sum_{i\in g}M_i.\]
\[\begin{gathered}\pi_{i\mid G}=\frac{M_i}{M_g}\\[5pt] i\in g\in G.\end{gathered}\]

If \(T_i\) is a PSU total, the conditional estimator is

\[\begin{gathered}\hat T_h=\sum_{g\in G}\frac{M_g}{M_{i_g}}T_{i_g}\\[5pt] E(\hat T_h\mid G)=\sum_iT_i.\end{gathered}\]

For independent PPS choices across groups, its conditional variance is

\[\begin{gathered}V(\hat T_h\mid G)\\=\sum_g\left\{M_g\sum_{i\in g}\frac{T_i^2}{M_i}-T_g^2\right\}\end{gathered}\]

with \(T_g=\sum_{i\in g}T_i\). This is small when totals are close to proportional to size. Because the conditional expectation is the same total for every partition, unconditional variance is the expectation of this conditional variance. Random grouping spreads selections across distinct groups and avoids drawing the same PSU twice. A group-conditional probability is different from an inclusion probability averaged over random partitions.

Historical LFS implementation: 2017 §2.6.2 uses six rotation groups with approximately equal numbers of PSUs, selecting one per group by PPS. Equations 2.3–2.7 use controlled, rounded inverse sampling ratios \(ISR_i^*\) as the effective size measure, proportional to household counts before rounding. Within a group,

\[\pi_{i\mid G}=ISR_i^*/\sum_{k\in g}ISR_k^*\]

and the dwelling probability conditional on the PSU is \(1/ISR_i^*\). Their product is \(1/\sum_{k\in g}ISR_k^*\); controlled rounding makes the group sums common within the stratum. Thus the historical design is self-weighting within a stratum, before adjustments.

The 2017 method combines RHC with growth updates and coordinated random starts. The 2025 guide confirms two-stage cluster/dwelling sampling but does not name RHC or confirm the controlled-rounding and random-start machinery. The historical algorithm is not asserted here as the current production algorithm.

SSUs: selecting dwellings, then identifying usual residents

A list is extracted from the SBgR for each selected cluster, and a dwelling sample is selected from that list. A building can contain many dwellings. After selection, demographic information is collected for everyone who normally resides in the dwelling, with labour force information for eligible civilian residents aged 15+. The household roster is not a further independent sample of persons. The 2017 systematic random-start method supplies historical detail; the 2025 guide does not specify the precise current second-stage selection algorithm.

\[\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned}\pi_{hij}&=P(i\in s_h)\\&\quad\times P(j\in s_{hi}\mid i\in s_h)\\&=\pi_{hi}\pi_{hj\mid i}\end{aligned}\\[5pt] w_{hij}^{(0)}=\frac1{\pi_{hij}}.\end{gathered}\]

Index h identifies a stratum, i a unique PSU within h and j a dwelling within i. The multiplication is the conditional-probability identity, not independence. It assumes the dwelling can be reached only through that PSU, with positive, known selection probabilities. A person reached through that dwelling shares its base inclusion probability, subject to eligibility and frame rules. Final weights also account for growth, nonresponse, coverage and calibration.

A fully worked synthetic two-stage example

Use two strata, each with four PSUs. Form two fixed teaching selection groups per stratum; choose one PSU by PPS in each group, independently. In each selected PSU draw 10 distinct dwellings by SRS without replacement. This is a grouped PPS illustration, not a reconstruction of the LFS. Frame sizes are exact, all sampled dwellings respond, and there are no certainty units.

Prince Edward Island: one stage, still stratified

The 2025 guide explicitly selects dwellings from complete address lists for all PEI strata. There is no fictitious first-stage geographic PSU draw. For a simple one-stage SRS illustration, \(\pi_{hj}=n_h/N_h\) and \(w_{hj}^{(0)}=N_h/n_h\); this formula illustrates SRS, not the undocumented exact 2025 list algorithm. PEI still has the provincial six-month dwelling rotation.

The 2017 §2.5.6 explains the rationale: short travel distances and greater telephone collection reduced the operational benefit of clustering, while a sufficiently complete dwelling list enabled one-stage selection. Avoiding clustering could improve efficiency without increasing an already high sampling rate. The 2025 guide confirms one stage and a sample cap to limit burden, but does not repeat every historical rationale or super-stratum rule.

Why the design matters for uncertainty

Neighbours may share local industries, transport access or employment shocks. Positive intracluster correlation can make many interviews in one PSU less informative than interviews spread over many PSUs. With equal cluster size m and exchangeable correlation ρ, \(DEFF_{cluster}\approx1+(m-1)\rho\). Stratification may offset part of that loss. For a stated SRS benchmark, \(n_{eff}\approx n/DEFF\). Neither is a universal LFS constant. Continue to the two-stage variance decomposition and design-effect calculator.

Intermediate · 04

The six-month rotating sample

In the provinces, a dwelling stays for six consecutive months. One panel leaves each month and a new panel enters: five-sixths of the dwelling sample overlaps with the previous month. This is a design overlap fraction, not a correlation of 5/6 between final estimates. Changes in occupants, responses, outcomes, weights and calibration matter.

\[\begin{aligned}V_d(\hat Y_t-\hat Y_{t-1})&=V_d(\hat Y_t)+V_d(\hat Y_{t-1})\\&\quad-2\operatorname{Cov}_d(\hat Y_t,\hat Y_{t-1}).\end{aligned}\]
Derive the identity and interpret covariance

Center the estimates: \(A=\hat Y_t-E_d\hat Y_t\), \(B=\hat Y_{t-1}-E_d\hat Y_{t-1}\). Then

\[\begin{aligned}E_d[(A-B)^2]&=E_d[A^2]+E_d[B^2]\\&\quad-2E_d[AB]\end{aligned}\]

This exact algebraic identity does not require normality. If errors in the two months move together, differencing cancels part of them. Negative covariance does the opposite.

Writing covariance as \(\rho SE_tSE_{t-1}\) gives

\[\begin{gathered}v_\Delta=SE_t^2+SE_{t-1}^2\\\phantom{v_\Delta={}}-2\rho SE_tSE_{t-1},\\SE_\Delta=\sqrt{v_\Delta}.\end{gathered}\]

Equal SEs of 25,000 with \(\rho=0.7\) produce an SE of 19,365, versus 35,355 under independence. These are synthetic inputs. Coordinated bootstrap replicates can preserve real cross-month dependence.

Try the covariance slider in Laboratory C. Prefer the officially published SE of change where available; do not estimate a correlation from the dwelling overlap alone.

2025 Guide, §4 rotation; 2017 Methodology, §7.2.2 coordinated bootstrap.

Monthly interviews and the route to publication

The reference week normally contains the 15th day of the month; collection takes place over the following ten days. In-person and telephone interviews use computerized questionnaires; eligible households may self-complete a secure electronic questionnaire in English or French. Computer assistance is a feature of interviewing, not a separate fourth response mode. A knowledgeable household member usually answers for others: the 2025 guide reports about 65% proxy information. Every eligible person need not be interviewed separately.

Interviewers make reasonable repeat contact attempts, appointments and follow-ups. Nonresponse never authorizes substitution of a different dwelling. This differs from scheduled rotation. First interviews are usually in person and subsequent ones usually by telephone; some households may first confirm contact details in a brief initial survey (Guide Appendix C). Interviewer supervision and monitoring support consistent collection.

Encrypted responses travel over a secure connection to head office. Processing includes validation, editing, imputation, industry and occupation coding, derivation of labour status, weighting and composite calibration. Estimates then undergo seasonal adjustment where applicable, statistical quality assessment and confidentiality review before dissemination. These processes overlap operationally; the animated visual is a teaching sequence rather than a timetable for every production task.

2025 Guide §5, p. 24; §6, pp. 25–28; §7, pp. 29–34; Appendix C, p. 49.

Processing · missing information is also uncertain

Imputation, Missing Data, and the Statistical Consequences of Nonresponse

A complete record can contain estimated answers. Completeness is not evidence that every field was observed or that its unknown truth has been recovered.

The current LFS documents manual, deterministic, carry-forward and donor item imputation. Manual work addresses crucial or complicated demographics such as age or economic family structure. Completed records are checked again for validity and consistency. Deterministic imputation: information that implies an answer

Write observed answers as \(x_i\), the missing field as y and a valid logical rule as \(g\). An illustrative rule for a jobholder with no actual work and a qualifying vacation absence is

\[\begin{gathered}B_i=\{\text{job}_i=1,\ H_i=0,\\ A_i=\text{vacation}\},\\[6pt]\tilde y_i=\begin{cases}\text{employed, absent},&B_i\text{ holds},\\\text{unresolved},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{gathered}\]

For job=Yes, actual hours=0, absence=Vacation, this rule determines the status without borrowing a donor. It is a simplified application of official classification, not a rule that every zero-hour person is employed. The guide’s worked editing example reconciles a report of work with a later report of no hours to an employed-but-absent status. Wrong observed inputs can still propagate into a deterministic value.

Carry-forward: borrow from the same person's past

\[\begin{gathered}\tilde y_{i,t}=y_{i,t-1}\\[5pt]\text{valid, applicable prior value}.\end{gathered}\]

For example, an earlier usual-hours value of 35 can fill an unresolved current usual-hours field where the rule is appropriate. The 2025 guide names wages and usual work hours as common carry-forward fields. Persistence is a working assumption: actual hours and labour status may change rapidly. Carrying forward 35 when the unknown current value is 20 gives an error of 15; the old answer is not proof of the new one. Separately, the guide documents carrying initial labour responses for people aged 70+ through their subsequent five provincial interviews to reduce burden.

Donor imputation: compatible observed records

Define an illustrative donor class

\[\begin{gathered}\mathcal D_i=\{k:R_k=1,\ k\ne i,\\ C(x_k)=C(x_i)\}\end{gathered}\]

where R indicates an observed valid value and C maps observed characteristics to a matching class. A selected compatible record \(d(i)\in\mathcal D_i\) supplies

\[\begin{gathered}\tilde y_i=y_{d(i)}\\[5pt] y_i^{imp}=R_i y_i+(1-R_i)\tilde y_i.\end{gathered}\]

In a teaching class with donor usual-hours values 30, 35 and 40, a uniformly selected donor has conditional expected value 35 and donor variance \((25+0+25)/3=16.67\). Receiving 35 says nothing definitive about the recipient’s true answer. The 2025 guide documents donors with similar characteristics. The 2017 §§5.3.1–5.3.2 describe random hot-deck selection within classes, donor eligibility, validity/consistency checks and collapsing sparse classes. Their exact historical variable order and minimum-donor rules are not asserted as the current implementation.

Whole-record donors and total nonresponse

Under the 2025 guide, total nonresponse with known household demographics, usually from earlier collection, is treated by whole-record donor imputation. Matching uses previous-cycle labour information plus geography and demographics. If household demographics are unavailable, total nonresponse is handled by a nonresponse weight adjustment. Replacing a labour information vector \(\tilde{\mathbf y}_i=\mathbf y_{d(i)}\) is different from filling just one field. It preserves combinations of labour answers from a donor without establishing the recipient’s true combination. The 2017 §5.3.3 clarifies that donor and recipient records in its longitudinal system were person records even for household nonresponse.

Imputation and weighting are related, distinct operations

\[\begin{gathered}\hat T_{imp}=\sum_{i\in s} w_i\{R_i y_i+(1-R_i)\tilde y_i\}\\[5pt] a_i=\frac{w_i^{(0)}}{\hat r_{g(i)}}.\end{gathered}\]

The first equation describes an imputed total with the appropriate final weights; the second is a simple response-cell adjustment illustration, not the full LFS weighting algorithm. A response adjustment needs responding and missing outcomes to be sufficiently comparable within adjustment information. Calibration balances known auxiliary totals and composite information but cannot guarantee correction for differences within cells. The actual routing of cases determines which adjustments apply; multiplying every record by all adjustments indiscriminately can double count compensation. The next section develops calibrated estimation.

Bias, variance and assumptions about missingness

For fixed weights and complete-data values, the discrepancy caused by item imputation is

\[\begin{gathered}\hat T_{imp}-\hat T_{complete}\\=\sum_i w_i(1-R_i)(\tilde y_i-y_i)\end{gathered}\]

Its expectation is zero only under suitable assumptions about recipients, donors, logical rules or response processes. Missingness associated with the unobserved outcome can leave bias even after all fields are completed.

\[\begin{aligned}V(\hat T_{imp})&=V_{s,R}\{E_D(\hat T_{imp}\mid s,R)\}\\&\quad+E_{s,R}\{V_D(\hat T_{imp}\mid s,R)\}.\end{aligned}\]

This law-of-total-variance decomposition treats sampling s, response R and stochastic donor selection D as sources of randomness under a specified joint framework; conditioning includes available observed information. It is an analytical decomposition, not a claim that the LFS reports each component separately. Shared donors can create covariance; deterministic or carry-forward rules have no random donor-selection component but can still introduce errors and bias. Treating imputed values as independently and fully observed may underestimate uncertainty. Proper replication needs to reflect relevant imputation and weighting steps, or an appropriate model-based adjustment; the 2017 §7.3 describes limits on what its production replicates repeated.

MCAR, MAR and MNAR: general analytical frameworks

With X fully observed, \(Y_{obs}\) observed outcomes and \(Y_{mis}\) missing outcomes, MCAR means \(P(R\mid X,Y)=P(R)\). MAR means

\[\begin{gathered}P(R\mid X,Y_{obs},Y_{mis})\\=P(R\mid X,Y_{obs})\end{gathered}\]

MNAR means residual dependence on \(Y_{mis}\) remains after observed information is conditioned upon. MAR alone is not enough: donor classes or outcome/response models must use relevant information and have overlap. These are general concepts, not assertions that a specific LFS procedure assumes one mechanism. MNAR generally requires sensitivity analysis or additional information.

Regression imputation and multiple imputation are possible teaching extensions, but are not claimed here as official LFS item-imputation methods. Multiple imputation can propagate specified model uncertainty across completed datasets; it still needs survey design, proper models and missingness assumptions. Neither it nor donor imputation removes all nonsampling error.

Intermediate · 05

Weights turn observations into population estimates

\[\hat T_{HT}=\sum_{k\in s}\frac{y_k}{\pi_k}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal U}I_k\frac{y_k}{\pi_k}.\]

\(I_k\) is the indicator that unit k is selected. Since \(E_d I_k=\pi_k\), linearity of expectation gives \(E_d\hat T_{HT}=\sum_k y_k=T\). The Horvitz–Thompson total is exactly design unbiased when the frame covers the target population, selection probabilities are correct and positive, and the required outcomes are observed without error. Independence of selections is unnecessary.

Base weightInverse inclusion probability, with applicable design adjustments.
Nonresponse adjustmentInflate responding weights within adjustment groups to represent missing households.
CalibrationChoose final weights that reproduce auxiliary population controls.

The LFS uses more than a raw HT estimator. The 2025 guide describes subweights after design/nonresponse adjustments, calibration to demographic controls and composite estimation using past-month information. The 2025 guide explicitly confirms composite calibration and integrated weighting, with a common final person weight within a household; the 2017 method supplies deeper derivations. The exact 2017 implementation should not be presumed unchanged in every detail.

Calibration, nonresponse assumptions and ratio linearization

Calibration chooses \(w_k\) close to subweights \(a_k\), subject to \(\sum_{s_r}w_kx_k=X\), where \(x_k\) contains auxiliary characteristics and X contains their control totals. A quadratic criterion is \(\min_w\sum_{s_r}(w_k-a_k)^2/a_k\). Accurate controls and association between x and y can improve precision; constraints alone do not guarantee removal of bias.

An adjustment \(a_k=d_k/\hat r_g\) within group g relies on response being ignorable for the outcome after conditioning on adjustment information, or sufficiently similar responding/nonresponding outcomes within the group. Unknown response mechanisms can violate this. Calibration may correct known demographic imbalance while leaving bias within demographic cells.

\[\begin{gathered}\hat R=\frac{\hat T_y}{\hat T_x}\\[5pt] \begin{aligned}\hat R-R&\approx\frac{\hat T_y-T_y}{T_x}\\&\quad-R\frac{\hat T_x-T_x}{T_x}.\end{aligned}\end{gathered}\]

Expand g(a,b)=a/b at (T_y,T_x): its derivatives are 1/T_x and −R/T_x. Thus

\[\begin{aligned}V_d(\hat R)&\approx T_x^{-2}\bigl[\\&\quad V_d(\hat T_y)+R^2V_d(\hat T_x)\\&\quad-2R\operatorname{Cov}_d(\hat T_y,\hat T_x)\bigr]\end{aligned}\]

Estimate this using linearized residuals \(z_k=y_k-\hat R x_k\) and the actual design. Ratios are generally not exactly design unbiased; the expansion needs a stable nonzero denominator and small relative fluctuations.

Composite calibration adds auxiliary information derived from overlapping units and previous-month estimates. It can improve levels and changes when this information is predictive. It is not equivalent to taking an unweighted average of two months.

Synthetic unequal-weight example. Two unemployed people have weights 100 and 100; two employed people have weights 400 and 400. The unweighted unemployment rate is 50%, but \(\hat U=200,\hat E=800,\hat L=1,000\), so the weighted rate is 20%. The same final weights must enter numerator and denominator.

Statistical foundations · 06

A sampling distribution describes estimates we could have obtained

Hold the finite population fixed and repeatedly apply the same probability design. Each sample gives an estimate. Their distribution is the sampling distribution. Its spread is the sampling standard deviation, the theoretical standard error. In practice, we estimate that spread from one survey sample using design information.

Sampling error\(\hat\theta-\theta\) for a realized sample; usually unobservable.
Bias\(E_d\hat\theta-\theta\), a property over samples.
Precision versus accuracyLow variance means precision. Accuracy also depends on bias and other errors.
\[\begin{aligned}\operatorname{MSE}_d(\hat\theta)&=E_d[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]\\&=V_d(\hat\theta)\\&\quad+\{E_d\hat\theta-\theta\}^2.\end{aligned}\]

Write the error as \((\hat\theta-E_d\hat\theta)+(E_d\hat\theta-\theta)\), square it and take expectations. The cross term vanishes because the centered estimator has expectation zero. RMSE is the square root of MSE. The identity is exact when the second moment exists.

For SRS at a small sampling fraction, SE typically decreases like \(n^{-1/2}\): quadrupling n halves SE. A larger sample cannot by itself cure nonresponse or measurement bias. Effective sample size summarizes a variance comparison for a specific estimator and outcome; it is not a universal count of “independent respondents.”

Advanced · 07

Variance must follow the design

Simple random sampling without replacement

\[\begin{gathered}\widehat V_d(\bar y)=\left(1-\frac nN\right)\frac{s^2}{n}\\[5pt] s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k\in s}(y_k-\bar y)^2.\end{gathered}\]

Here n distinct units are sampled uniformly from N; \(n\ge2\). With population variance \(S^2=(N-1)^{-1}\sum(y_k-\bar Y)^2\), the exact design variance is \((1-n/N)S^2/n\). The sample s² is unbiased for S² under this design. The finite population correction reflects that observing a large fraction of a fixed population leaves less uncertainty; at n=N the variance is zero.

Derive SRS and stratified variance

Under SRS, \(\pi_k=n/N\) and \(\pi_{kl}=n(n-1)/[N(N-1)]\) for distinct units. Substitution in the HT variance and division by N² gives the stated mean variance. Negative covariance between inclusion indicators is what produces the finite population correction.

\[\begin{gathered}\hat T_{st}=\sum_hN_h\bar y_h\\[5pt] \widehat V_d(\hat T_{st})=\sum_hN_h^2(1-f_h)\frac{s_h^2}{n_h}\\[5pt] f_h=\frac{n_h}{N_h}.\end{gathered}\]

Independent SRS samples are drawn without replacement within disjoint strata h, with known N_h and n_h≥2. Each stratum total is unbiased. Independence across strata removes cross-stratum covariance; multiplying a stratum mean by N_h multiplies its variance by N_h². For a mean divide total variance by \(N^2\). These formulas do not describe LFS clustered, calibrated estimation.

For fixed total n and equal unit cost, minimize \(\sum_hN_h^2S_h^2/n_h\) with a Lagrange multiplier. Differentiation gives \(n_h\propto N_hS_h\) (Neyman allocation). Domain-quality constraints, costs and respondent burden alter the optimum; this is a benchmark, not the actual 2025 allocation algorithm.

General unequal-probability totals

\[\begin{gathered}V_d(\hat T_{HT})=\sum_k\sum_l(\pi_{kl}-\pi_k\pi_l)\frac{y_k}{\pi_k}\frac{y_l}{\pi_l}\\[5pt] \pi_{kk}=\pi_k.\end{gathered}\]

This follows by expanding the variance of \(\sum I_ky_k/\pi_k\) and using \(\operatorname{Cov}(I_k,I_l)=\pi_{kl}-\pi_k\pi_l\). An unbiased sample variance estimator replaces each summand by its sampled counterpart divided by \(\pi_{kl}\), when those pairwise probabilities are positive and known. Designs with zero pairwise inclusion require special treatment.

Two-stage decomposition, clustering and linearization
\[\begin{aligned}V_d(\hat T)&=V_1\{E_2(\hat T\mid s_1)\}\\&\quad+E_1\{V_2(\hat T\mid s_1)\}.\end{aligned}\]

The law of total variance separates variability in selecting clusters (stage 1) from sampling dwellings inside selected clusters (stage 2). Suppose cluster i has total T_i and an unbiased within-cluster estimate \(\hat T_i\) of variance V_i. For \(\hat T=\sum_{i\in s_1}\hat T_i/\pi_i\) and independent second-stage samples across clusters, the second term is \(\sum_i V_i/\pi_i\); the first is the HT variance of the true cluster totals. Calibration and nonlinear rates add complexity.

With equal cluster size m, exchangeable outcome variance σ² and within-cluster correlation ρ_c, the variance of a sum of m observations is \(m\sigma^2+m(m-1)\rho_c\sigma^2\). Relative to m independent observations this gives \(1+(m-1)\rho_c\). This model approximation ignores finite population correction, unequal cluster sizes, strata, weights and calibration.

Taylor linearization turns a smooth nonlinear estimator into an approximate linear statistic; replication instead recalculates the nonlinear statistic using perturbed weights. Both must retain the sample's clustering and stratification. One-person iid formulas do neither.

\[\begin{gathered}DEFF(\hat\theta)=\frac{V_{design}(\hat\theta)}{V_{SRS}(\hat\theta)}\\[5pt] n_{eff}\approx\frac n{DEFF}.\end{gathered}\]

The denominator needs an explicitly matched SRS benchmark for the same target, outcome and nominal n. The effective-size relation follows when the benchmark variance is approximately proportional to 1/n. It becomes less exact with substantial sampling fractions, complicated ratios or changing benchmarks. Stratification can yield DEFF<1; weighting and clustering often push it upward. Laboratory D keeps a user-specified DEFF separate from a clustering-only approximation.

Theory derived here; see the Methodology bibliography for Cochran (1977) and Särndal, Swensson and Wretman (1992).

Advanced · 08

Bootstrap Variance Estimation under Complex Survey Designs

Ordinary iid resampling of person records discards geographic clustering, unequal probabilities and cross-month dependence. A survey bootstrap modifies weights according to the actual selection structure and relevant weighting steps. Each replicate must recalculate the statistic—including both numerator and denominator for a rate.

\[\widehat V(\hat\theta)=c\sum_{b=1}^{B}(\hat\theta^{(b)}-\theta_{centre})^2.\]

B is the number of replicate estimates, c the procedure-specific scaling and \(\theta_{centre}\) the prescribed centering value. Neither c nor the centering is universal. This generic expression is not claimed as the exact current LFS production formula.

What is documented, and when? The 2017 methodology reports that bootstrap replaced jackknife in January 2015. It describes 1,000 Rao–Wu replicates, resampling clusters within strata (dwellings in PEI), coordinated samples across months and replicate composite calibration. Its equation 7.2 uses the replicate mean as center and 1/B scaling. The 2025 guide confirms bootstrap-weight access, but does not re-document every production algorithm or confirm that every 2017 rescaling detail survived the redesign. We retain that distinction.
Historical Rao–Wu rescaling and the 2017 LFS implementation

In a stratum with n_h sampled PSUs, draw n_h−1 PSUs with replacement. Let \(m_{hj}^{(b)}\) count selections of PSU j. The initial rescaled weight is \(w_{hjk}^{(b)}=[n_h/(n_h-1)]m_{hj}^{(b)}w_{hjk}\). Resampling PSUs rather than independent persons preserves within-PSU dependence.

The 2017 Chapter 7 explains the with-replacement approximation to the actual PPS without-replacement selection. It notes that only the final composite calibration step was repeated in that production system, rather than every preceding adjustment. It describes adapted resampling for special cases and coordination when PSUs change. This page's teaching bootstrap does not implement those exceptions.

\[\begin{gathered}\widehat V_{BOOT,2017}=\frac1B\sum_b(\hat\theta^{*(b)}-\bar\theta^*)^2\\[5pt]\bar\theta^*=\frac1B\sum_b\hat\theta^{*(b)}.\end{gathered}\]

The actual design, replicate-weight documentation and estimator must be used together. A household-level weight file needs proper linkage to all eligible persons; missing or unmatched records can change the estimand.

Public-use files are not a substitute for master-file design-aware replicate weights. The 2025 guide locates bootstrap weights in secure Research Data Centre master files for approved projects. It also describes Real Time Remote Access as a separate access mechanism; do not assume it provides downloadable replicate weights. Custom SEs/CVs can be requested on a cost-recovery basis.

2017 Methodology, §§7.1–7.4; 2025 Guide, §7 and §9 access to microdata; Research Data Centres.

Ordinary iid bootstrap as a benchmark

For n independent identically distributed observations, draw n indices independently with replacement from their empirical distribution. For a mean, \(\bar y^{(b)}=n^{-1}\sum_{r=1}^n y_{I_r^{(b)}}\). Conditional on the data, \(V_*(\bar y^{(b)})=n^{-2}\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2\), approximately \(s^2/n\). This is a useful iid benchmark, but independently redrawing survey persons breaks household/PSU dependence, stratum allocation and the connection between selection probabilities and weights.

Replicate totals and ratios

\[\begin{gathered}\hat T_y^{(b)}=\sum_{i\in s}w_i^{(b)}y_i\\[5pt]\hat R^{(b)}=\frac{\sum_{i\in s}w_i^{(b)}y_i}{\sum_{i\in s}w_i^{(b)}x_i}\\[5pt]\widehat V=c\sum_{b=1}^B(\hat\theta^{(b)}-\hat\theta^\star)^2.\end{gathered}\]

B counts replicates, \(\hat\theta^{(b)}\) is the statistic recomputed with replicate weights, \(\hat\theta^\star\) is the prescribed center and c is the scheme-specific multiplier. Some methods center at the original estimate; others at the replicate mean. Replicate-weight documentation determines both. A ratio needs both totals recalculated, with a nonzero replicate denominator. For a change, use the same coordinated replicate identifier across months before computing variance.

Deriving a specified rescaled-cluster illustration

Assume independent strata with \(n_h\ge2\) equally selected PSUs, negligible first-stage sampling fractions, and complete observed PSU summaries. Treat within-PSU contributions as fixed in this teaching approximation. Draw \(n_h-1\) sampled PSUs with replacement within each stratum. Multiplicities \(m_{hi}^{(b)}\) follow a multinomial distribution with \(n_h-1\) trials and equal probabilities \(1/n_h\). Let \(a_{hi}=\sum_j w_{hij}y_{hij}\) denote a weighted PSU contribution.

\[\begin{gathered}w_{hij}^{(b)}=\frac{n_h}{n_h-1}m_{hi}^{(b)}w_{hij}\\[5pt] E_*m_{hi}=\frac{n_h-1}{n_h}\\[5pt] \begin{gathered}V_*\!\left(\sum_i\frac{n_h}{n_h-1}m_{hi}a_{hi}\right)\\=\frac{n_h}{n_h-1}\sum_i(a_{hi}-\bar a_h)^2.\end{gathered}\end{gathered}\]

The expectation reproduces each original PSU contribution. Using multinomial variance and negative covariance yields the final expression, equal to the familiar with-replacement PSU variance estimate \(n_h s_{a,h}^2\). Independent strata add variance contributions. This derivation concerns totals; ratios use replicate recalculation or residual linearization. It does not establish validity for arbitrary PPS, large sampling fractions, certainty PSUs, lone-PSU strata or missing second-stage variation. Those cases need suitable design-specific adaptations. Here the Monte Carlo variance uses the replicate mean with c=1/B, matching the historical convention; c=1/(B−1) is the unbiased finite-B sample-variance alternative for independent simulated replicates.

Confidence intervals: method and target must agree

\[\begin{gathered}SE_{boot}=\sqrt{\widehat V}\\[5pt] CI_{normal}=\hat\theta\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}SE_{boot}\\[5pt] CI_{percentile}=[Q_{\alpha/2}(\hat\theta^{(b)}),Q_{1-\alpha/2}(\hat\theta^{(b)})].\end{gathered}\]

A normal interval requires an adequately approximated sampling distribution, small bias and enough independent design information. Few PSUs, rare outcomes and unstable denominators can make it unreliable; a design-based t reference or another supported method may be preferable. The simulation reports a nominal illustrative 95% normal interval with no validated coverage claim for this eight-PSU sample.

Percentile endpoints summarize replicate quantiles and can reflect skewness, but rescaled replicate distributions are not automatically calibrated for percentile coverage. Bias, boundaries, transformations and the replication design matter. We show percentile quantiles only as descriptive endpoints, not an official LFS confidence interval. Published standard errors and design-supported approximations should guide real LFS inference.

Numerical checks for the teaching simulation compare browser variance with exact enumeration of its small synthetic distribution.

Advanced · 09

Ordinary CV and the 2025 adjusted CV

\[\begin{gathered}CV(\hat\theta)=\frac{SE(\hat\theta)}{|\hat\theta|}\\[5pt] CV_{adj}=CV\sqrt{\frac p{1-p}}\sqrt{\frac{1-q}{q}}.\end{gathered}\]

CV expresses relative sampling precision. A count of 100,000 with SE 5,000 has CV 0.05, or 5%. CV becomes unstable near zero and is not a useful headline measure for a signed monthly difference. Adjusted CV is a design-comparison measure, not an interval or an observed SE.

For the guide's unemployment-count quality targets, \(p=U/P\) and \(q=0.045\). This p is the proportion of the population aged 15+ who are unemployed, distinct from the unemployment rate \(u=U/L\) and from the participation p used in Section 2. Here p follows the guide's notation solely within the adjusted-CV discussion.

Derive the adjustment and its limitations

For an SRS Bernoulli proportion, ignoring the sampling fraction, \(SE(\hat p)\approx\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\), hence \(CV\approx\sqrt{(1-p)/(np)}\). Multiplying by the adjustment cancels the dependence on p, giving \(CV_{adj}\approx\sqrt{(1-q)/(nq)}\). With a fixed DEFF, replace n by n/DEFF. Calibration, nonresponse and a DEFF that varies with the outcome prevent exact invariance.

If a calibrated population total P is treated as fixed, the count U and proportion U/P have the same CV; the adjustment can therefore be extended to unemployment counts. This requires the appropriate in-scope denominator. Holding SE fixed while changing p will not make adjusted CV constant: that would violate the Bernoulli variance relationship used to motivate cancellation.

For p=0.04, q=0.045 and SE(p)=0.002, CV=5% and adjusted CV=

\[\begin{gathered}0.05\sqrt{\frac{0.04}{0.96}}\sqrt{\frac{0.955}{0.045}}\\=4.702\%\end{gathered}\]

The SE is still 0.002, or 0.2 percentage points. The adjustment does not replace it.

Statistical inference · 10

Confidence intervals measure a procedure's coverage

\[CI_{1-\alpha}\approx\hat\theta\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{SE}(\hat\theta).\]

For a sufficiently regular estimator with an approximately normal standardized sampling distribution and a reliable SE, critical values are about 1.645, 1.960 and 2.576 at 90%, 95% and 99%. The margin of error is the critical value times SE. Counts use count units; rates displayed in percent require SEs in percentage points.

A fixed parameter does not have a 95% frequentist probability of being in one realized interval. Rather, under repeated sampling and the stated assumptions, approximately 95% of intervals constructed by this procedure contain that parameter. A realized interval either covers or does not. The sampling design and estimator determine the validity of this statement.

Counts, proportions, ratios and degrees of freedom

A symmetric normal interval for a sparse count can extend below zero. A proportion interval can extend beyond [0,1]. Truncating endpoints improves appearance but does not validate coverage. Boundary-aware transformations or design-appropriate methods may be necessary. An iid Wilson interval is not automatically a valid complex-survey interval.

Ratios require variance of the ratio, not numerator variance alone. If the denominator is noisy or near zero, linearization and a symmetric interval can fail. For smaller design degrees of freedom, a survey t critical value may be appropriate; an often-used degrees-of-freedom starting point is sampled noncertainty PSUs minus strata, subject to the method and domain. The relevant degrees of freedom are not simply the person sample size. Published SEs do not by themselves expose all ingredients for exact small-sample inference.

All official-data intervals on this page use an explicitly stated large-sample normal approximation. They incorporate published sampling SEs, not a separately estimated total-survey-error distribution.

\[Z=\frac{\hat\theta_t-\hat\theta_{t-1}}{\widehat{SE}(\hat\theta_t-\hat\theta_{t-1})}.\]

Under a zero-change null and the normal approximation, reject at two-sided 5% when |Z|>1.96, equivalently when the 95% change interval excludes zero. The SE must be for the difference and account for covariance. A level interval is not a change interval; comparing whether two level intervals overlap is not the correct difference test.

Statistical and economic meaning differ. A precise but tiny change may have little policy consequence. A large imprecise estimate may remain economically important to investigate. Failure to reject zero does not prove no change. Repeatedly testing many provinces and indicators also creates a multiplicity problem; the exploratory case table uses unadjusted individual intervals.

2025 Guide, §7 standard errors, confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

Total survey error · 11

A narrow sampling interval can still miss systematic error

The 2025 guide documents response rates falling during the pandemic and only partly recovering. It describes added sample to support quality in this environment. This does not establish a specific September 2026 response rate or provincial bias; none is invented here. Response rate is an operational measure, not a direct measurement of bias.

Why nonresponse weighting cannot guarantee no bias

Suppose a synthetic population contains 500 employed and 500 unemployed people. All employed respond, but only half of unemployed respond. Among 750 respondents the unweighted unemployment rate is 250/750=33.3% although the truth is 50%. A single adjustment cell inflating all respondent weights by 1,000/750 preserves 33.3% and leaves bias of −16.7 percentage points.

Increasing the size of a sample with the same response pattern narrows a conventional sampling interval around the wrong centre. Auxiliary variables that distinguish response propensities and outcomes can reduce the problem, but broad demographic calibration alone may not identify it. This is a nonresponse mechanism example, not evidence of an LFS bias of that magnitude.

Linked administrative studies, follow-up studies, frame evaluation and interviewer/processing audits can investigate error components. They require data beyond the public aggregate table. Imputation replaces missing values, not uncertainty: the variance estimator should reflect the implemented imputation/weighting procedure to the extent documented.

2025 Guide, §§4–7; 2017 Chapter 5 processing and imputation; Chapter 8 data quality.

Time-series interpretation · 12

Seasonal adjustment changes the question being asked

Unadjusted data include regular seasonal hiring and labour-supply patterns. Seasonally adjusted data aim to make successive months more comparable by removing estimated seasonal and calendar effects. The 2025 guide identifies X-12-ARIMA methodology implemented through X-13ARIMA-SEATS and SAS X12/X13—not an assertion that every series uses a SEATS decomposition.

National employment and unemployment are aggregated from adjusted major age/gender groups. Other breakdowns are independently adjusted and reconciled to benchmarks by raking. Provincial and national consistency may require further reconciliation. The adjustment is a statistical estimation process; recent observations and endpoint forecasts influence components.

Annual seasonal-adjustment revisions incorporate new observations and revised factors. Other historical revisions can reflect population controls or methodological updates. Revision risk is distinct from sampling variance. A confidence interval from published seasonally adjusted SEs does not predict later revisions. Use the matching published SE rather than inventing an adjustment factor for seasonality.

2025 Guide, §6 seasonal adjustment and revisions; US Census Bureau, X-13ARIMA-SEATS documentation.

Resample three clusters with replacement per stratum and multiply their multiplicities by 4/3. Recalculate the weighted rate; variance is the mean squared deviation from the replicate mean. No calibration, nonresponse, finite-population correction or LFS production exceptions are modelled.

Worked mathematics · 16

Ten complete calculations

All values in this section are synthetic. Each example states the assumptions needed to interpret its arithmetic.

1 · SRS mean and finite population correction

Assume SRS without replacement: N=10,000, n=400, s²=0.0736 for a binary outcome. Then

\[\begin{aligned}\widehat V(\bar y)&=\left(1-\frac{400}{10,000}\right)\frac{0.0736}{400}\\&=0.00017664\end{aligned}\]

so SE=0.0132906, or 1.329 percentage points. Ignoring the 4% sampling fraction gives SE=1.356 points. This is mean variance; total variance would multiply by N².

2 · Weighted unemployment count

Assume complete observed outcomes and correct inverse probabilities. Unemployed units have inclusion probabilities 0.01 and 0.02. Their count contribution is \(1/0.01+1/0.02=150\). An employed person contributes zero to U. This calculation does not supply a variance: pairwise probabilities or replicate weights are also required.

3 · Ratio unemployment rate

Two unemployed weights 100 each and two employed weights 400 each give Û=200 and L̂=1,000. Hence \(\hat u=200/1,000=20\%\). Assume estimated variances V(Û)=400, V(L̂)=2,500 and covariance 500. Linearization gives

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{\begin{gathered}400+0.2^2(2,500)\\-2(0.2)(500)\end{gathered}}{1,000^2}\\=0.0003\end{gathered}\]

SE≈1.732 points. The covariance is essential. These moments are synthetic, not inferred from four records alone.

4 · Stratified total and variance

Independent SRS within two strata: N₁=1,000, n₁=100, mean₁=0.10, s₁²=0.09; N₂=2,000, n₂=200, mean₂=0.20, s₂²=0.16. Total estimate=1,000(0.10)+2,000(0.20)=500. Variance=

\[\begin{aligned}\widehat V&=\frac{1,000^2(0.9)0.09}{100}\\&\quad+\frac{2,000^2(0.9)0.16}{200}\\&=810+2,880\\&=3,690\end{aligned}\]

Total SE=60.745; mean SE=60.745/3,000=0.020248.

5 · Design effect and effective size

For a specified estimator let design variance=0.00016 and matched SRS variance=0.00008. DEFF=2. At nominal n=1,000, effective n≈500 under 1/n variance scaling. Separately, equal clusters of m=20 with ICC=0.1 give clustering DEFF≈1+19(0.1)=2.9. It would be inconsistent to call both values the same design effect.

6 · Ordinary and adjusted CV

For U/P=0.04 and SE=0.002, ordinary CV=0.002/0.04=0.05. At q=0.045, adjusted CV=

\[\begin{gathered}0.05\sqrt{\frac{0.04}{0.96}}\sqrt{\frac{0.955}{0.045}}\\=0.0470185\end{gathered}\]

or 4.702%. If P is fixed at 1,000,000, U=40,000 and SE(U)=2,000 give the same CV. This p is not U/L.

7 · A 95% count interval

Estimate=100,000, SE=5,000. Under a large-sample normal approximation, margin=1.96×5,000=9,800 and interval=[90,200,109,800]. Its repeated-sampling coverage is approximately 95%; it is not a 95% posterior probability for the fixed count. It excludes nonsampling bias.

8 · A difference with covariance

Current=1,030,000, previous=1,000,000, both SE=25,000, assumed correlation=0.7. Covariance=0.7×25,000²=437,500,000. Difference variance=1,250,000,000−875,000,000=375,000,000; SE=19,364.9. The 95% interval is 30,000±37,955.2=[−7,955.2,67,955.2], with Z=1.549. It includes zero. Independence would give SE=35,355.3; the real survey change SE must be sourced rather than guessed.

9 · A simplified replicate variance

Suppose an illustrative resampling procedure yields estimates 0.09,0.10,0.11,0.10. Their mean is 0.10. With c=1/B=1/4 and replicate-mean centering, variance=(0.0001+0+0.0001+0)/4=0.00005; SE=0.007071, or 0.7071 points. Four replicates are far too few for a stable real survey variance; the example isolates the formula. Other procedures can prescribe other c and centers.

10 · Bias outside a sampling interval

True unemployment=50%; employed response probability=1, unemployed response probability=0.5. Respondents have unemployment=1/3. Suppose a naive large sample reports SE=0.01: its normal interval is [31.37%,35.29%], nowhere near 50%. The small SE does not measure the −16.67-point nonresponse bias. The MSE framework must include the systematic error relative to the target.

Interpretation · 17

What can we confidently conclude?

Probability sampling makes the LFS an instrument for statistical inference. Known selection rules and design-aware estimation allow us to quantify how results would vary over repeated samples. This measurable uncertainty is a strength of the survey, not a reason to treat it as inherently unreliable.

Complex design matters because clustered, unequal-weight, calibrated observations carry different information from independent random people. National and provincial precision differ, and changes require covariance-aware SEs rather than level SEs. Published confidence intervals would still be incomplete descriptions of total error because coverage, response, measurement and revisions require separate evaluation.

For each release, check the target population, period, units and adjustment status; use the right indicator denominator; match the SE to the level or change; note rounding and data vintage; consider the interval alongside economic magnitude; and investigate nonsampling error and revision context. A nonzero reported movement is an estimate. A significant test is evidence under assumptions. Neither alone is a causal policy explanation.

Compact glossary

Estimand
The specific population quantity to be estimated.
Estimator
A rule mapping a sample to an estimate.
Inclusion probability
The probability a unit is selected under the sample design.
Stratum
A defined group sampled separately.
PSU / cluster
A first-stage sampling unit containing lower-stage units.
Calibration
Adjusting weights to reproduce auxiliary control totals.
Standard error
The sampling SD of an estimator, usually itself estimated.
CV
SE divided by the absolute estimate, a relative-precision measure.
Design effect
Variance relative to an explicitly defined SRS benchmark.
Replicate weight
A perturbed survey weight used to recalculate an estimate.
Coverage probability
The fraction of repeated-sampling intervals containing the truth.

An absolute difference between rates stated in percent.
