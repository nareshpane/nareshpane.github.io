Part I

The experiment

A clinical question, its documented design and the corrected outcome definitions.

01 · The clinical question

Can a synbiotic prevent sepsis or death?

A lower infection rate among infants who receive a supplement could reflect the supplement, healthier families choosing it, better access to care, or different recognition of illness. A randomized comparison changes the source of treatment assignment: chance supplies the comparison group. The question becomes whether assigning the same eligible population to one strategy rather than another changes its outcomes.

This monograph develops the existing visual RCT guide through the trial by Pinaki Panigrahi and colleagues in rural Odisha, India. The investigators tested a seven-day oral synbiotic against placebo, with surveillance through 60 days of age. The outcome was a composite of sepsis/possible severe bacterial infection (pSBI) or death. A composite event is counted once; it does not require both sepsis and death.

02 · Intervention and mechanism

One preparation, several biological hypotheses

Probiotic

A live microorganism intended to confer a health benefit when administered adequately. Effects depend on the strain, dose and setting.

Prebiotic

A substrate used by host microorganisms with a proposed health benefit. Here, fructooligosaccharide (FOS) accompanied the organism.

Synbiotic

A combination of microorganisms and substrate. This trial compared the combination with placebo; it cannot isolate the contribution of each component.

The deposited protocol specifies Lactobacillus plantarum ATCC 202195 (5 mg, approximately 1–2 × 109 colony-forming units), FOS 150 mg and maltodextrin 345 mg per capsule. The placebo contains maltodextrin alone. The powder was to be reconstituted in 2 mL of 5% dextrose saline and given orally once daily for seven days. These are historical protocol specifications, not instructions for use.

The proposed pathway involves colonization, competition with pathogens and host defenses. Neither a lower clinical event rate nor evidence from preceding colonization studies proves which pathway produced the observed contrast. A factorial trial of organism and substrate would answer a different question.

Protocol pp. 24–26. Organism name follows the original trial. The educational definitions above do not establish efficacy of other products.

03 · Protocol and conduct

Randomized infants, monitored within communities

The protocol called for individual 1:1 assignment using computer-generated blocks of four within villages/Anganwadi centers. The UMB BioCore was to generate the list; allocation information was restricted to the BioCore and clinical trial supplier. Coded bottles and study kits separate sequence generation from administration. The reporting summary says field personnel, hospital caregivers and investigators were blinded. This describes the documented design; the public data cannot audit every concealment or masking decision.

Published eligibility included birth weight at least 2,000 g, gestation at least 35 weeks and no signs of sepsis or other morbidity. Protocol exclusions also address inability to feed, antibiotics, major anomalies and maternal signs of infection. The deposited document is V2, July 15, 2008, with amendments appended. Amendment 1 (July 20, 2007) changes data-management responsibility from RTI to GCRC. The supplement’s CONSORT checklist reports no changes after trial commencement, while these pilot-to-main-trial amendments are documented. Keep that timing distinction visible.

Daily home assessment included feeding, temperature and illness signs, with referral to study hospitals and clinical/microbiological evaluation when indicated. A. Foundations

Causal questions need a comparison

A descriptive question asks what happened. A causal question asks what would differ if the same eligible people were assigned one intervention rather than another. The protocol should state the population, intervention, comparator, outcome, follow-up, and target effect before outcome data are examined.

C. Foundations

Eligibility, consent, and the target population

Eligibility criteria define who could be randomized. Recruitment and consent determine who actually enters. The randomized comparison identifies an effect for the enrolled trial population under its protocol; transport to another population requires a separate judgment about populations, setting, implementation, and follow-up.

Baseline is pre-treatment. It may describe who enrolled and support a planned precision adjustment. It is not an outcome caused by assignment.

Trial connection. The enrolled infant population excludes many of the most premature, smallest or already ill newborns. Randomization does not remove that eligibility boundary.

Mathematical bridge · identification

From chance assignment to an unbiased contrast

Define Zi as randomized assignment (1 synbiotic, 0 placebo), and Yi(z) as an indicator of the primary adverse event by day 60 under assignment z. Thus a negative difference is beneficial. The preserved migraine example below uses a favorable-response indicator, so its sign reverses. Outcome coding is part of the estimand.

$$Y_i^{obs}=Z_iY_i(1)+(1-Z_i)Y_i(0),\qquad \tau_N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\{Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\}.$$

Consistency links the assigned strategy to its observed outcome. A no-interference assumption says one infant’s assignment does not change another infant’s outcome. Well-defined treatment versions matter: storage, formulation and delivery are part of the strategy. Randomization makes the assignment independent of the potential outcomes under the implemented allocation design; recruitment itself is not random sampling from all infants.

Derivation under complete randomization

For fixed group sizes n1, n0 and N = n1 + n0, every infant has probability n1/N of treatment. Hold potential outcomes fixed and average over permitted assignments:

$$E_Z(\bar Y_1^{obs})=\frac1{n_1}\sum_i E_Z(Z_i)Y_i(1)=\frac1N\sum_iY_i(1).$$ $$E_Z(\widehat\tau)=\tau_N,\qquad \widehat\tau=\bar Y_1^{obs}-\bar Y_0^{obs}.$$

This is design-based unbiasedness across assignments, not guaranteed equality in a realized sample. The finite-population randomization variance is:

$$\operatorname{Var}_Z(\widehat\tau)=\frac{S_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{S_0^2}{n_0}-\frac{S_\tau^2}{N},\quad S_z^2=\frac{\sum_i(Y_i(z)-\bar Y(z))^2}{N-1}.$$

Sτ2 is the variance of individual effects, unobserved because joint potential outcomes are unobserved. Omitting its nonnegative term gives the usual conservative Neyman variance estimator using observed group variances. The following complete-randomization labs are synthetic; the actual trial used village-specific blocks of four. A design-faithful permutation test would preserve those blocks rather than freely shuffle all 4,556 labels.

Exchangeability is not automatic in observational comparisons

Health-seeking behavior could affect both receiving an intervention and infection detection in observational data. Random assignment breaks that pre-assignment dependence in distribution. Conditioning on treatment receipt, survival to a selected follow-up visit or a post-assignment mediator can introduce new selection. Covariate adjustment cannot recover concealed assignment or missing outcomes without additional assumptions.

F. Randomization and balance

Balance is probabilistic, not identical

Randomization balances measured and unmeasured pre-treatment factors in expectation. Any one allocation can have differences by chance. A baseline table should describe the randomized groups; a small imbalance does not by itself show failed randomization, and significance tests of baseline differences do not validate a randomization procedure.

Design protection

Conceal the sequence, document the algorithm, and retain the allocation record.

Analysis protection

Use all assigned participants in the primary assigned-group comparison.

Trial connection. Compare the synthetic balance lab with the released-data baseline table in Part IV. Neither is a test of successful concealment.

G. Randomization and balance

Blocking and stratification

Permuted blocks can keep group sizes close during enrollment. Stratified randomization applies a separate assignment schedule within important baseline strata, such as site. Small fixed blocks can be predictable if allocation is not concealed; variable block sizes reduce that risk. Analyze stratification factors when appropriate and account for the actual design.

Trial connection · The actual trial used fixed blocks of four within villages. Variable blocks in general design discussions are an alternative, not a claim about this trial.

H. Potential outcomes

Each person has two potential outcomes

Let $Y_i(1)$ be participant $i$'s outcome under treatment assignment and $Y_i(0)$ the outcome under control assignment. The individual causal effect is:

$$\tau_i=Y_i(1)-Y_i(0).$$

For this binary endpoint, each potential outcome is 1 for positive and 0 for negative. The average treatment effect in a finite randomized population is $\tau=N^{-1}\sum_i[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)]$.

Coding reminder · “Positive” means favorable response only in the fictional migraine examples. For the infant primary endpoint, Y=1 is an adverse event, and a negative assignment effect is beneficial.

Mathematical bridge · binary outcomes

Derive the effects from four counts

Let a and b be synbiotic events and non-events; c and d placebo events and non-events. Then n1 = a+b, n0 = c+d, and estimated risks are p̂1 = a/n1 and p̂0 = c/n0. For an adverse event:

$$\widehat{RD}=\hat p_1-\hat p_0,\quad \widehat{ARR}=\hat p_0-\hat p_1,\quad \widehat{RR}=\frac{\hat p_1}{\hat p_0},\quad \widehat{RRR}=1-\widehat{RR},\quad \widehat{OR}=\frac{a/b}{c/d}.$$

Relative reduction is a fraction of the baseline risk; absolute reduction is a difference in probabilities. The odds ratio compares odds rather than risks. It can differ materially from RR when events are common. NNT = 1/ARR describes the reciprocal of a population risk difference over this follow-up horizon; it does not identify which individual infant benefits.

Delta-method derivation of a log-risk-ratio interval

Under an independent-binomial approximation, Var(p̂z) ≈ pz(1−pz)/nz. For g(p)=log p, g′(p)=1/p. Therefore Var(log p̂) ≈ (1−p)/(np). Add the two group variances and plug in observed proportions:

$$\widehat{SE}\{\log(\widehat{RR})\}=\sqrt{\frac1a-\frac1{n_1}+\frac1c-\frac1{n_0}}.$$ $$CI_{RR}=\exp\left[\log(\widehat{RR})\pm z_{.975}\widehat{SE}\{\log(\widehat{RR})\}\right],\qquad z_{.975}\approx1.96.$$

This Katz interval is positive and asymmetric on the RR scale. It assumes enough events for the normal approximation and positive event counts. Zero cells need another method; this guide does not silently add half-events. Small mortality counts make the approximation fragile. Blocked assignment, dependence, repeated looks and uncertainty about missing endpoints call for additional analysis.

Risk-difference uncertainty and reciprocal intervals
$$\widehat{SE}(\widehat{RD})=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat p_0(1-\hat p_0)}{n_0}},\quad CI_{RD}=\widehat{RD}\pm z_{.975}\widehat{SE}.$$

A Wald interval is an approximation and can behave poorly for rare events or probabilities near boundaries. If an ARR interval [L,U] is wholly positive, transform it to the NNT interval [1/U,1/L]. If it includes zero, a single finite benefit-only NNT interval is misleading: the reciprocal includes infinity and may extend into harm.

M. Uncertainty

Sampling and randomization uncertainty

Repeated trials or repeated assignments would not give the same estimate. A common large-sample standard error for a difference in independent proportions is:

$$SE(\widehat\tau)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)}{n_1}+\frac{\hat p_0(1-\hat p_0)}{n_0}}=0.0424.$$

This calculation describes uncertainty under a model-based large-sample approximation. Its adequacy depends on the design, outcome, sample size, and analysis; clustered or repeated observations need corresponding methods.

O. Testing

Hypothesis tests address a stated null

For a sharp null of no effect or a model-based null of equal risks, a test statistic compares the observed difference with values expected under that null. A two-sided large-sample z statistic using the pooled null proportion is approximately 3.37 here. A p-value is the probability, under the chosen null and procedure, of a statistic at least as discordant as observed. It is not the probability that the null is true and does not measure effect size.

Synthetic educational example
P. Testing

Fisher randomization inference follows the assignment rule

Under Fisher's sharp null, every participant's outcome is unchanged by assignment. Hold the observed outcomes fixed, repeatedly reassign exactly 270 treatment labels according to the actual randomization scheme, and compare each simulated difference with the observed 0.1444. The randomization p-value is the share at least as extreme, with a prespecified convention for ties. For 540 people, enumerating all $\binom{540}{270}$ assignments is impractical; a reproducible Monte Carlo sample can approximate the randomization distribution.

Scope matters. Fisher's sharp null is stronger than a null of zero average effect. Randomization inference is most credible when it mirrors the allocation, blocking, and analysis statistic actually used.

Q. Planning

Power is a design property

Power is the probability that a prespecified procedure rejects a stated null when a chosen data-generating scenario holds. It depends on sample size, allocation ratio, outcome variability or risk, target effect, alpha level, clustering, missingness, adherence, and the planned analysis. It is not the probability that a completed positive result is correct.

R. Planning

Plan sample size from a meaningful scenario

Specify a minimally important effect and plausible control risk, then calculate the required sample size for the primary estimand and test. Inflate deliberately for anticipated loss to follow-up or design effects rather than hoping the observed sample will compensate. Sensitivity calculations across plausible assumptions are more informative than a single optimistic input.

Mathematical bridge · testing and planning

Distinguish a null model, an assignment design and a stopping rule

For independent binary outcomes, the pooled null risk under H0: p1=p0 is p̂=(a+c)/(n1+n0). The uncorrected two-sided Pearson test is equivalent to squaring the pooled z statistic:

$$z=\frac{\hat p_1-\hat p_0}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)(1/n_1+1/n_0)}},\quad X^2=z^2,\quad p=2\Phi(-|z|).$$

A Fisher exact test conditions on margins in a two-by-two table. Fisher randomization inference instead refers to the actual assignment mechanism under the sharp null Yi(1)=Yi(0) for every infant. The unrestricted synthetic shuffle below is a lesson in that principle; it is not the trial’s blocked randomization test.

Power before outcomes, not a probability of truth afterward

The original planning target was 8,442 infants, 4,221 per arm, to detect a reduction from 8% to 6.4% at two-sided α=0.05 with 80% power. Those assumptions are verified in the protocol and reporting summary. The protocol also discusses interim looks and O’Brien–Fleming boundaries; the supplement says recruitment ended in 2012 after a DSMB recommendation for early efficacy.

Normal approximation used in the redesigned power explorer

For equal n per group and planning risks p0, p1=p0−δ, let p̄=(p0+p1)/2. Approximate the rejection threshold using the null variance and the sampling distribution using the alternative variance:

$$c=z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)/n},\quad s_A=\sqrt{\{p_0(1-p_0)+p_1(1-p_1)\}/n},$$ $$\operatorname{Power}\approx\Phi\left(\frac{-c+\delta}{s_A}\right)+1-\Phi\left(\frac{c+\delta}{s_A}\right).$$

This continuous approximation need not return exactly the historical planning number; it omits discreteness, loss allowance and sequential monitoring. Sample-size software choices can differ. It is not a post-hoc certainty score for the completed trial.

Early stopping matters. Our reported intervals and p-values use fixed-sample formulas. The public release does not include the sequence of interim analysis datasets, information fractions or DSMB decision record needed to reproduce sequentially adjusted inference. A match to the published rounded RR does not reproduce the entire monitoring procedure.

S. Estimation

Covariate adjustment can improve precision

Pre-treatment prognostic covariates can make an estimate more precise when modeled appropriately. State the adjustment set and method before analysis, preserve the assigned-group estimand, and use uncertainty estimates compatible with the model and randomization. Do not adjust for post-randomization variables when the goal is the total effect of assignment; they can lie on the causal pathway or introduce bias.

Trial connection. The protocol proposed regression and stepwise selection if imbalances appeared. This replication uses crude risks to keep the primary reconstruction inspectable; no post-treatment feeding or adherence covariates are adjusted for.

T. Estimation

Multiplicity changes what a result means

Many outcomes, time points, subgroup tests, or interim looks create more opportunities for chance findings. Designate primary outcomes and analyses, distinguish confirmatory from exploratory work, and state how families of tests or sequential looks will be handled. A transparent exploratory analysis can be useful without being presented as a definitive confirmation.

Trial connection. The trial’s multiple morbidity comparisons and early monitoring require care. The reconstructed forest intervals are pointwise, unadjusted intervals.

There are 4,554 distinct identifiers: two identifiers are repeated across four rows, with no exactly duplicated records. Replication 05 · follow-up time

Event age alone cannot recover a survival curve

TIME is documented as age of event. It is recorded for 704 morbidity records and absent for most other infants. Of 329 primary events, 327 have an event age. Infants without primary events do not all have last-contact or censoring dates, and individual entry ages are not supplied.

What a Kaplan–Meier analysis would additionally need
$$\widehat S(t)=\prod_{t_j\le t}\left(1-\frac{d_j}{r_j}\right).$$

dj is the number of events at time tj; rj is the number under observation and event-free just before it. Last-contact times determine who remains in rj. With enrollment after birth, an age-scale analysis also needs entry ages for delayed entry. Inventing day-60 censoring for all missing times would silently assume complete observation for lost infants. The binary released-endpoint analysis answers a narrower, reproducible question.

Exploratory · effect modification

Compare effects with an interaction contrast

One documented baseline split is infant sex. The exploratory contrast is the male RR divided by the female RR. This directly compares effects on a multiplicative risk scale, rather than comparing whether two subgroup p-values cross 0.05.

$$\theta=\log(RR_{male})-\log(RR_{female}),\quad SE(\hat\theta)\approx\sqrt{SE_{male}^2+SE_{female}^2}.$$

How many unascertained events would change the estimate?

Among 129 synbiotic and 82 placebo records flagged LOSTFU, all are coded non-events. Assign an assumed event fraction to each flagged group. These assumptions are not newly observed outcomes.

Assuming an event for every flagged infant in both arms gives RR 0.875 (95% CI 0.747–1.026), similar to the published supplementary scenario. Allowing all flagged synbiotic infants to have events and none of the flagged placebo infants does reverse the point estimate. Thus the supplement’s equal-percentage scenarios should not be generalized into robustness to every differential missingness pattern.

The supplement describes later parental tracing of lost infants without formal physician examination at the primary horizon. That supports context but cannot be treated as identical prospective ascertainment. Neither the release nor this analysis identifies the true unobserved outcomes.

Conduct, extensions and credibility

Preserved explanations of blinding, attrition, adherence, instruments, heterogeneity, clusters and factorial designs.

U. Conduct

Blinding reduces differential behavior and assessment

Blinding can apply to participants, care providers, outcome assessors, analysts, or adjudicators. Look-alike tablets may help maintain participant and provider blinding, while a centralized outcome procedure may blind assessors. Blinding is not a binary label: describe who was blinded, what information was masked, and how emergencies or unblinding were handled.

W. Adherence

Intention-to-treat keeps the randomized comparison

Noncompliance includes not starting, stopping, switching, or using nonstudy treatment. The intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis compares participants according to randomized assignment, regardless of what they later received. It estimates the effect of an assignment strategy under the trial's follow-up and is protected by randomization. Excluding nonadherers or analyzing by received treatment can break that protection because post-randomization behavior may be related to prognosis.

Trial connection. The all-assigned reconstruction uses 2,278 records per arm; it preserves assignment while sensitivity analysis addresses ascertainment.

X. Adherence

CACE targets compliers under extra assumptions

The complier average causal effect (CACE) asks about people who would take treatment if assigned treatment and not take it if assigned control. Assignment can serve as an instrumental variable only with assumptions such as random assignment, a relevant effect of assignment on receipt, exclusion of direct assignment effects beyond receipt, and a stated monotonicity condition. These assumptions are substantive and generally cannot all be verified from trial data alone.

$$\text{CACE}\approx\frac{\text{ITT effect on outcome}}{\text{ITT effect on treatment receipt}}$$

Trial connection. No CACE estimate is claimed from this deposit. Dose totals alone do not identify principal adherence strata.

Y. Extensions

Heterogeneity asks whether effects vary

Average effects can coexist with variation across participants or contexts. Credible subgroup analyses are prespecified, limited in number, based on an interaction contrast rather than separate within-subgroup p-values, and reported with uncertainty. A subgroup finding is not established merely because one subgroup crosses a conventional threshold and another does not.

Trial connection. The actual sex interaction is shown in Part IV with a direct ratio-of-risk-ratios contrast.

Z. Extensions

Cluster trials randomize groups, not people

Schools, clinics, or communities may be randomized when intervention delivery is naturally shared. People within a cluster tend to be correlated, reducing the information supplied by a fixed number of people. A common planning approximation is the design effect $1+(m-1)\rho$, where $m$ is cluster size and $\rho$ an intracluster correlation. Analysis must account for clustering and the number of randomized clusters, not treat all observations as independent.

Trial connection. The synbiotic trial individually randomized infants within village blocks. The cluster trial illustration describes a different design.

AA. Extensions

Factorial trials study more than one intervention

A 2-by-2 factorial trial randomizes two factors, creating four assignment cells. It can estimate each main effect efficiently when the design and estimand support averaging across the other factor. An interaction asks whether one intervention's effect changes with the other; it should be planned and powered separately rather than assumed absent.

AD. Checklist

Before calling an RCT result persuasive

  • Is the causal question and estimand explicit?
  • Were eligibility, recruitment, and consent described?
  • Was allocation genuinely random and concealed?
  • Were groups analyzed by assigned treatment?
  • Are outcome definitions and timing prespecified?
  • Are group numerators and denominators reported?
  • Are effect size and uncertainty shown on a useful scale?
  • Does inference match the randomization and analysis?
  • Are missing data and protocol deviations transparent?
  • Are subgroup and multiplicity issues handled honestly?
  • Are blinding, clustering, or interference considered?
  • Are ethical limits and generalizability stated?
Core idea: randomization supports a causal assigned-group comparison. Careful measurement, complete follow-up, transparent analysis, and disciplined interpretation determine how useful that comparison remains.
Interpretation · scope of the evidence

What this experiment supports

The reported assigned-group comparison supports a lower risk of the sepsis/pSBI-or-death composite under this particular synbiotic strategy among eligible enrolled infants in this setting. The released records reproduce the main binary numerator contrast and the rounded primary RR interval. The reduction is primarily in morbidity; the trial does not establish a reduction in mortality alone.

Supported by this replication

Documented arm coding, randomized denominators, primary event totals, major component totals and a transparent unadjusted binary analysis. Relative and absolute reductions describe the same observed comparison.

Still unresolved

Follow-up-field discrepancies, some variable definitions, exact original CI software, individual censoring histories and the sequential decision process. Neither mechanism nor universal applicability follows from numerical agreement.

External relevance depends on eligibility, gestational age, birth weight, infection ecology, strain, formulation, storage, delivery, background care and surveillance. Randomization does not automatically make a convenience enrolled population representative of very preterm infants, other regions or current clinical practice. This guide evaluates a historical experiment and is not advice to administer a product.

Assignment, receipt, interference and implementation

The assignment effect includes the actual adherence and support available in the trial. The release records seven doses for 4,377 infants but does not identify causal effects among adherers. Per-protocol selection may be prognostic. A complier analysis needs a defensible treatment-receipt variable and additional exclusion and monotonicity assumptions; it is not attempted here. Within-household or community spillovers would complicate a no-interference interpretation even though the allocation unit was the infant. Village blocking is not cluster randomization.
