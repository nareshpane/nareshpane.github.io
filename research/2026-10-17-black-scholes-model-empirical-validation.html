01 / From proportional changes to a stochastic equation

% Intuition

A percentage shock should scale with the current price: the same +1% move adds $0.10 to a $10 stock and $10 to a $1,000 stock. Multiplying both components by S models proportional changes rather than a fixed dollar disturbance.

Separate a conditional mean contribution from an unpredictable shock. Over a short interval, steady drift accumulates in proportion to elapsed time. Independent random shocks accumulate in variance, so their standard deviation grows with the square root of elapsed time.

S, μ, σ Symbols
\(S=S_t\)
Current underlying asset price at time t.
\(\Delta S\)
Price change \(S_{t+\Delta t}-S_t\) over the interval.
\(\mu\)
Instantaneous price drift under the physical measure, per year; not the risk-free rate.
\(\sigma\)
Annualized diffusion volatility, entered as a decimal (20% = 0.20).
\(\Delta t\)
A small elapsed time measured in years; dt is differential time notation.
\(Z\sim N(0,1)\)
A standard normal random variable. N(0,1) denotes the normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1.
\(W_t\)
Brownian motion: continuous paths and independent, centered normal increments.
\(\Delta W\)
Brownian increment \(W_{t+\Delta t}-W_t\); mean 0, variance Δt.
\(dS,\ dW\)
Stochastic differential notation for price and Brownian increments in the continuous-time model.
ΔS Equation
\[\Delta S\approx\underbrace{\mu S\Delta t}_{\text{conditional mean / drift}}+\underbrace{\sigma S\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z}_{\text{random diffusion}},\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).\]

With S held at its current value for this small-step approximation, the first term is determined by the model inputs. The second term is random: σS√Δt sets its scale, while Z supplies its sign and size.

√Δt Derivation

1 · Match the shock’s variance. Brownian increments over disjoint intervals are independent, so their variances add: an interval twice as long has twice the variance, not twice the standard deviation. Because Var(Z) = 1, choosing \(\Delta W=\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\) gives:

\[\mathbb E[\Delta W]=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\Delta W)=\Delta t,\qquad\operatorname{SD}(\Delta W)=\sqrt{\Delta t}.\]

2 · Substitute the Brownian increment. This writes the same small-step approximation as a drift contribution plus diffusion:

\[\Delta S\approx\mu S\Delta t+\sigma S\Delta W.\]\[\mathbb E[\Delta S\mid S_t=S]\approx\mu S\Delta t,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\Delta S\mid S_t=S)\approx\sigma^2S^2\Delta t.\]

3 · Specify the continuous-time model. The corresponding stochastic differential equation is:

\[\boxed{dS=\mu Sdt+\sigma SdW.}\]
What does the differential equation mean precisely?

It represents an integral equation, with a time integral and an Itô stochastic integral:

\[S_t=S_0+\int_0^t\mu S_u\,du+\int_0^t\sigma S_u\,dW_u.\]

The discrete expression evaluates S at the start of each short interval. Refining that construction gives this continuous-time model under the usual conditions. It is not an ordinary differential equation obtained by differentiating a smooth random path.

dS Interpretation

The price’s local expected change scales with Δt, while the random component’s typical magnitude scales with √Δt. Brownian motion is almost surely nowhere ordinarily differentiable: dW/dt is not an ordinary derivative. The stochastic equation describes increments and their integral accumulation.

Model assumptions. Constant μ and σ, continuous prices, and Brownian innovations are an idealization. Actual jumps and changing volatility are excluded here. The drift is physical-measure price growth; risk-neutral pricing replaces it with r − q only later in the replication argument.

An ordinary return is \(R_i=S_i/S_{i-1}-1\); a log return is \(x_i=\log(S_i/S_{i-1})\). Log returns add across time. Brownian motion \(W_t\) has independent Gaussian increments with \(\Delta W\sim N(0,\Delta t)\). Its paths are continuous, yet almost surely nowhere differentiable.

\[\frac{\Delta S}{S}\approx\mu\Delta t+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z,\quad Z\sim N(0,1)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad dS=\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW.\]

The drift \(\mu\) gives instantaneous expected price growth, and diffusion \(\sigma\) scales random shocks. Itô’s correction changes log growth: \(d\log S=(\mu-\sigma^2/2)dt+\sigma dW\). Consequently \(S_t=S_0\exp[(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma W_t]\). This geometric Brownian motion stays positive and has a lognormal terminal distribution.

From Randomness to Black–Scholes

Follow the logic from a changing stock price to a replicating strategy and a price.

01 / Why value depends on stock price and time

Intuition

A right to buy becomes more useful as the stock rises. It also changes as the deadline approaches, even if the stock is unchanged.

Symbols

V(S,t) is the option value, τ its fixed expiration, and T = τ − t its remaining maturity. Subscripts denote partial derivatives.

\(V_S=\partial V/\partial S\) is sensitivity to spot with time fixed; \(V_{SS}=\partial^2V/\partial S^2\) is curvature in spot; \(V_t=\partial V/\partial t\) is change with current time while spot is held fixed.

Equation
\[V=V(S,t),\qquad dS=\mu Sdt+\sigma SdW^P.\]
Derivation

For smooth V, a second-order expansion starts with \(\Delta V\approx V_t\Delta t+V_S\Delta S+\frac12V_{SS}(\Delta S)^2\). A random stock increment is of order √Δt, so its square is of order Δt and cannot be discarded alongside higher-order terms.

Interpretation

Time and price are separate state coordinates. The formula assumes constant r, q and σ; if these were additional stochastic state variables, this two-variable description would need expansion.

02 / Itô’s lemma keeps the curvature term

Intuition

A curved function responds differently to up and down shocks even when the shocks average to zero. Curvature creates a drift correction.

Symbols

Itô rules: (dW)² = dt in quadratic variation, dt dW = 0, and (dt)² = 0. V_S is delta and V_SS is gamma.

Equation
\[dV=V_tdt+V_SdS+\tfrac12V_{SS}(dS)^2\]\[=\left(V_t+\mu SV_S+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}\right)dt+\sigma SV_SdW^P.\]

Why does a second derivative appear?

The stock increment is of order √dt. Its square is of order dt, so curvature contributes at the same time scale as ordinary drift.

Derivation
A concrete example: apply the rule to log S

For f(S) = log S, f′ = 1/S and f″ = −1/S². Insert both derivatives, not only the first:

\[d\log S=\frac{1}{S}(\mu Sdt+\sigma SdW)-\frac{1}{2S^2}\sigma^2S^2dt\]\[=(\mu-\sigma^2/2)dt+\sigma dW.\]

Integrating gives \(\log(S_\tau/S_t)=(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T+\sigma(W_\tau-W_t)\). With μ = 6% and σ = 25%, log drift is 0.06 − 0.25²/2 = 0.02875, or 2.875% per year, even though expected price growth uses 6%.

Interpretation

The −σ²/2 term is the difference between mean log growth and instantaneous expected price growth. It is a consequence of curvature, not an arbitrary adjustment.

03 / Construct a hedge and cancel the random term

Intuition

The option and stock respond to the same local price shock. Choose the stock holding so their random exposures offset.

Symbols

Hold one option and short Δ = V_S units of stock. Π = V − ΔS is the marked hedged position. The short stock pays continuous dividends qSΔ dt.

The hedge ratio \(\Delta\) is a stock holding per option, distinct from the finite price change ΔS. \(\Pi\) is the marked portfolio value, while dG denotes its trading gain; the cash-account proof below explains the distinction.

Equation
\[dG=dV-\Delta(dS+qSdt)\]\[=\underbrace{\sigma S(V_S-\Delta)}_{0}dW^P+\left[V_t+\mu S(V_S-\Delta)+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}-qS\Delta\right]dt\]\[=\left(V_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}-qSV_S\right)dt.\]

Where did the randomness go?

The hedge cancels two exposures to the same Brownian increment. It does not predict or eliminate the stock’s movement.

Derivation
Holdings, rebalancing and the self-financing cash account

The gain equation uses the current holdings times asset total returns. Differentiating V − ΔS literally also produces −S dΔ − d[Δ,S]. A self-financing cash account funds hedge adjustments, offsetting these rebalancing terms. Treating the marked-value differential as the trading gain without that cash account is incorrect. The stock gain includes qS dt; a short stock position owes that dividend.

Interpretation

Both the Brownian risk and the physical drift μ cancel when Δ = V_S. This is a local infinitesimal hedge; discrete rebalancing does not cancel all realized risk.

04 / No arbitrage turns the hedge into a PDE

Intuition

A self-financing, locally riskless position must earn the financing rate. Otherwise it can be combined with borrowing or lending to create a local arbitrage under the model assumptions.

Symbols

The financing rate is r. The hedge value is Π = V − SV_S, and its locally riskless gain is rΠ dt.

Equation
\[V_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}-qSV_S=r(V-SV_S).\]

Why does the expected return disappear?

Setting Δ = V_S removes the μS(V_S − Δ) term along with the Brownian term. The financing condition then supplies r, not a forecast of the stock’s return.

Derivation
\[V_t+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}-qSV_S-rV+rSV_S=0\]\[\boxed{V_t+(r-q)SV_S+\tfrac12\sigma^2S^2V_{SS}-rV=0.}\]

The coefficient of SV_S is r − q because the hedge stock earns dividends as well as changing price.

Interpretation

The physical expected return μ is absent from the pricing PDE. Replication removes the stock risk locally; it does not prove that the stock’s actual expected return equals r − q.

05 / The contract supplies the terminal condition

Intuition

The PDE describes how value evolves before expiry. The promised payoff tells us which solution we need.

Symbols

C is a European call, P a European put; x⁺ means max(x, 0). Discount factors use remaining time T.

Equation
\[C(S,\tau)=(S-K)^+,\qquad P(S,\tau)=(K-S)^+.\]
Derivation

At zero spot, the call has no value and the put delivers K at expiry: C(0,t) = 0 and P(0,t) = Ke−rT. As S grows without bound, C approaches Se−qT − Ke−rT and P approaches zero. These conditions, with suitable growth conditions, select the economically relevant solution.

Interpretation

The payoff is a boundary in time, not the option’s present value. European exercise is essential; an American early-exercise problem has an additional free boundary.

06 / Use a pricing measure, not a return forecast

Intuition

The PDE can be represented as a discounted expectation under a measure that makes discounted total-return trading gains martingales.

Symbols

P denotes the physical measure (and, in price formulas, P also denotes a put; context distinguishes them). Q denotes the risk-neutral pricing measure; W^Q is Brownian motion under Q.

\(H(S_\tau)\) denotes the terminal payoff. \(\mathbb E^Q[\,\cdot\mid S_t=S]\) is a Q-weighted expectation conditional on today’s price. With constant continuously compounded r, \(e^{-rT}\) is the discount factor over T years.

Equation
\[dS=(r-q)Sdt+\sigma SdW^Q,\quad V=e^{-rT}\mathbb E^Q[H(S_\tau)\mid S_t=S].\]

What does risk-neutral really mean?

Distinguish a model for real-world price evolution from the measure that values replicated cash flows.

Derivation

Apply the same log transformation, now with drift r − q, then integrate the Brownian increment:

\[\log S_\tau=\log S+(r-q-\sigma^2/2)T+\sigma\sqrt T\,Z,\quad Z\sim N(0,1)\text{ under }Q.\]

This discounted expectation solves the terminal-value PDE by the Feynman–Kac representation. In particular, \(\mathbb E^Q S_\tau=Se^{(r-q)T}\).

Interpretation

Under the physical measure the price drift remains μ; under Q it is r − q. Risk-neutral probabilities are valuation weights. They are not measured real-world probabilities, and neither test estimates physical future stock-return probabilities.

07 / Evaluate the discounted call payoff

Intuition

Split the call payoff into the stock delivered when exercise is favorable and the strike paid in that same event.

Symbols

Let a = log S + (r − q − σ²/2)T, b = σ√T and z* = (log K − a)/b. Exercise occurs when Z > z*. φ is the standard normal density and N its CDF.

Precisely, \(N(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\phi(z)\,dz\): the probability that a standard normal variable is at most x. N(·) is a cumulative distribution function, distinct from the distribution notation N(0,1).

Equation
\[C=e^{-rT}\left[e^a\int_{z_*}^{\infty}e^{bz}\phi(z)dz-K\int_{z_*}^{\infty}\phi(z)dz\right].\]

How does an expectation become an option price?

Average the contractual payoff using the pricing distribution, then discount the future cash amount. The integral below performs that average explicitly.

Derivation
Complete the square: every integration step
\[e^{bz}\phi(z)=\frac{e^{-z^2/2+bz}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=e^{b^2/2}\phi(z-b).\]\[\int_{z_*}^{\infty}e^{bz}\phi(z)dz=e^{b^2/2}\int_{z_*-b}^{\infty}\phi(u)du=e^{b^2/2}N(b-z_*).\]\[\int_{z_*}^{\infty}\phi(z)dz=N(-z_*),\qquad e^{-rT+a+b^2/2}=Se^{-qT}.\]

Substitute these results into the expectation. Define d₂ = −z* and d₁ = b − z*; the first integral uses N(d₁) and the exercise indicator uses N(d₂).

Interpretation

The stock-weighted expectation and the exercise probability are different terms. That distinction explains why the formula needs both d₁ and d₂.

08 / Read the formula and recover the put

Intuition

The call equals a prepaid stock component minus a discounted strike component, each weighted by the appropriate normal probability.

Symbols

d₁ is a standardized exercise threshold shifted by σ√T; d₂ is the standardized risk-neutral log-price threshold. N(d₂) = Q(Sτ > K). N(d₁) is a stock-value-weighted probability, not the ordinary exercise probability.

Equation
\[d_1=\frac{\log(S/K)+(r-q+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T},\quad d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt T.\]\[\boxed{C=Se^{-qT}N(d_1)-Ke^{-rT}N(d_2).}\]\[\boxed{P=Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2)-Se^{-qT}N(-d_1).}\]

Two thresholds, two different weights

d₂ standardizes the exercise threshold under Q. The extra σ√T shift in d₁ appears when the expectation is weighted by the terminal stock value.

Derivation
Put integration and continuous-dividend put–call parity

For the put, integrate over Z < z*. The strike integral gives Ke−rTN(z*) and the stock integral gives Se−qTN(z* − b). Since z* = −d₂ and z* − b = −d₁, this gives the put formula above.

Pointwise, \((S_\tau-K)^+-(K-S_\tau)^+=S_\tau-K\). Take the discounted Q expectation:

\[C-P=e^{-rT}(Se^{(r-q)T}-K)=\boxed{Se^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT}}.\]

A prepaid forward delivering one stock unit costs Se−qT; subtracting the discounted strike replicates the call-minus-put payoff. Matched European exercise, maturity and settlement are required.

Interpretation

Call delta is e−qTN(d₁), so with dividends it is not simply N(d₁). The formula is exact under its assumptions; empirical accuracy depends on parameter choices and whether those assumptions describe the quotes.

Rates, dividends, volatility: three different decisions

Rates. We read the contemporaneous Treasury par curves in the sample manifest: 1 month, 3 months, 1, 2, 5, 10 and 30 years, in annual percent. Divide by 100, linearly interpolate yields to T, and hold the 1-month yield flat for shorter terms. Approximate continuous rates by \(r=2\log(1+y/2)\). This bond-equivalent conversion is an approximation to a discount curve, not a zero-coupon bootstrap. The 3-month nodes are checked against historical FRED DGS3MO; missing bond-market dates use the manifest’s documented carry-forward curves.

Dividends. No zero-dividend assumption is hidden. For each date–expiration, designated training strikes with 0.97 ≤ K/S ≤ 1.03 supply matched call–put midpoints. Compute \(F_j=K_j+e^{rT}(C_j-P_j)\), take their median, then \(q=r-\log(F/S)/T\). Require at least three pairs, six training quotes, and −10% < q < 15%. This is market-implied carry, absorbing dividends, financing and timing noise. Both tests therefore contain market calibration. A fixed 1.5% q sensitivity is an explicit scenario, not a verified ex-ante dividend forecast.

Volatility. Historical σ is the sample standard deviation (ddof = 1) of the preceding 20 daily SPX log returns, multiplied by √252; the 30-return estimator is a sensitivity check. The rolling estimate is shifted one trading date: a quote on date d uses returns ending on d−1, never the current or future return. Cboe’s earlier history supplies the warm-up. Calendar time / 365 and trading-day volatility / √252 have distinct roles.

A · Historical-volatility benchmark

06.3 / Where the Model Falls Short

The market’s skew and the model’s assumptions tell different stories.

Invert each valid European midpoint to get its implied volatility: the σ that makes BSM equal that midpoint under the chosen rate and carry. Our independent bracketed bisection uses 60 iterations over 0.000001–5.0, rejects bound violations and returns missing if the bracket cannot contain a solution. This is a description of market prices, never an independent pricing test.

The empirical volatility skew

Median independently inverted midpoint IV (% annualized), pooled across dates and option sides within K/S and maturity bins; at least 30 valid solves per cell. Different cells can contain different dates and contracts. This is a pooled skew summary, not a synchronized tradable volatility surface.

Volatility by maturity and moneyness

Distributional misspecification

A constant σ cannot reproduce a strike-dependent smile or downside skew. Equity-index protection can carry a different price than upside exposure. Stochastic volatility, leverage effects and jumps can generate tail shapes beyond a lognormal law. Their relative contribution cannot be identified from this comparison alone.

Implementation misspecification

Discrete component dividends differ from a smooth yield. Changing rates, asynchronous quotes, stale prices and wide spreads enter measured residuals. Discrete rebalancing, commissions, slippage, funding and index-tracking costs prevent the frictionless hedge from being a trading-profit guarantee.

06.4 / What Survives the Checks?

Numerical identities are testable; empirical conclusions remain conditional.

A small numerical audit

The 21 Python tests compare analytic Greeks against central finite differences for calls and puts, check the independent IV solver, pricing identities, quote filters and metric calculations. Vendor verification establishes archive integrity and documented consistency, not authenticity of every market quote.

The equation is exact within its assumptions; the empirical fit also depends on how volatility and carry are chosen. A calibrated fit can improve because it incorporates current option-market information. That improvement alone neither predicts future returns nor demonstrates a profitable strategy.

Scope of inference. This is one six-month 2022 sample of European SPXW contracts, with 7–90 day maturities and restricted moneyness. The repeated contracts and dates are dependent. The rate curve is a par-yield approximation; dividend carry is calibrated; quote times are unavailable. Selection removes cheap and poor-quality quotes. Results need not extend to American options, crisis jumps, other years or far-out-of-the-money tails. No historical hedge backtest or transaction-cost-adjusted trading test is claimed.

07.1 / Rights on a digital underlying

Bitcoin Options and Their Economic Purpose

A Bitcoin option separates the right to a future payoff from ownership of the asset today. A holder can limit the initial option outlay while taking exposure to price changes or uncertainty. A put can hedge a BTC holding; a call can express upside exposure. Neither position removes counterparty, liquidity or opportunity-cost risk.

For an illustrative $10,000 strike, a call has positive intrinsic value when the expiration reference price exceeds $10,000; a put has positive intrinsic value below it. The premium is paid before the outcome is known. Exercise at expiry, resale before expiry and profit after costs are different concepts.

07.2 / One claim, two units of account

How Deribit Bitcoin Options Work

Deribit’s 2019 explanation documents European exercise, automatic cash settlement and the BTC–date–strike–side identifier. Its January 2020 announcement identifies 08:00 UTC expiry and the BTC index. A 2020 call-options explanation explicitly converts dollar intrinsic value into BTC at settlement.

Those sources support the historical mechanics, not every specification of an uninspected record. The current specification states a one-BTC multiplier and describes a settlement-process change from August 2026. That later futures-intermediated process must not be retrofitted onto the earlier dataset. Historical multiplier, reference-index composition, delivery-price averaging and fee schedules still require matching to actual observation dates. Prices here exclude fees, funding and collateral effects.

Terminal payoff · one BTC of notional
\[H_T^{\mathrm{BTC},C}=\frac{(S_T-K)^+}{S_T},\qquad H_T^{\mathrm{BTC},P}=\frac{(K-S_T)^+}{S_T}.\]

Here \(S_T\) is the terminal conversion reference in USD/BTC and \(K\) is the dollar strike. Multiplying either BTC payoff by \(S_T\) gives its dollar-equivalent intrinsic value. Quantity and a verified contract multiplier scale the payment separately.

Why USD value divided by spot is a benchmark, not a universal inverse-option formula

Choose the USD money-market account as numeraire. If frictionless conversion at the terminal index is feasible, the BTC payment \(H_T^{\rm BTC}\) has terminal dollar value \(S_TH_T^{\rm BTC}\). For the payoff above this equals the conventional dollar intrinsic value. With a tradable spot, the stated financing assumptions and no basis or delivery frictions, its dollar value can therefore be modeled by a European formula; expressing that value in BTC now divides by \(S_t\).

\[V_t^{\rm USD}=e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb E^{\mathbb Q_{\rm USD}}[S_TH_T^{\rm BTC}\mid\mathcal F_t],\qquad V_t^{\rm BTC}=V_t^{\rm USD}/S_t.\]

\(\mathcal F_t\) denotes information available at time t. This is not the USD-risk-neutral expectation of the BTC payoff alone. In this expectation T denotes the expiration clock time; the calculator below uses T for the remaining year fraction. A BTC-numeraire expectation uses a different measure and financing asset. A nontradable delivery index, forward basis, lending yield, exchange collateral and fees can prevent the simple replication argument from representing the actual contract exactly.

07.4 / Independent inputs, explicit assumptions

Estimating the Model Inputs

Actual valuation spot: each record’s index_price. The separate history is an arithmetic mean of available Bitstamp, Bitfinex, Coinbase, itBit and Kraken prices; all 2,697,313 supplied averages reconcile. The history clock has no encoded timezone. 08 / Comparing Two Empirical InvestigationsSPXW versus BitcoinWhat two options markets reveal about Black–Scholes, and why their results require careful comparison.

How closely do prices predicted by Black–Scholes agree with actual option-market prices? This is the shared question. Comparing an equity index with a continuously traded digital asset shows how the same equation behaves with different inputs and market conventions.

One equation, two different tests. Both studies ask how far a theoretical option value sits from a recorded market price. The underlying assets, trading arrangements, liquidity, data sources, observation periods, premium units, volatility estimates and calibration procedures differ. They do not hold the market environment or information set constant. Read the comparison as a study of model inputs and experimental design, rather than a league table of assets.

SPXW · an equity index

July–December 2022 · 127 dates
238,836 held-out option quotes. European S&P 500 index options, valued against observed bid–ask midpoints.

The calibration uses selected contracts from the same date as the held-out contracts. Adjusting volatility with that day’s training quotes lowers average held-out pricing error. That is evidence of cross-sectional fit, not a test of predicting another day.

Bitcoin · a digital underlying

January 2019–February 2020 · 407 evaluation dates
178,236 transactions in 1,474 European BTC option contracts. BTC-denominated premiums; raw source coverage November 2016–February 2020.

The fitted volatility comes from earlier dates and stays fixed. It fits the later evaluation period worse than H30 or H60. This shows poor transfer for this specification and period; it does not establish that calibration is generally ineffective.

1 bp = 0.01 percentage point

Compare units first. A basis point (bp) is 0.01 percentage point. Mean absolute error divided by each observation’s underlying price, multiplied by 10,000, puts both studies on a common scale. It does not equalize their dates, quote quality, volatility windows or training information. A basis point here is a fraction of the underlying price, not of the option premium.

Design + limitations

A pricing error is the gap between a theoretical value and a recorded market price. Mean absolute error measures the size of that gap without allowing positive and negative errors to cancel. Changing volatility changes theoretical prices; it does not change the observed premium. Quote timing, executions, liquidity and financing conventions also affect what a comparison can establish.

The displayed Bitcoin calibrated error is larger than either historical-volatility error. In this implemented test, one volatility fitted to earlier observations transfers poorly to the later evaluation sample. No cause is isolated, and no universal failure of calibration follows. SPXW’s smaller reported errors likewise do not establish a universally superior fit: its input information and calibration design differ.

What changes from one market to the other?

Both samples use European options, but the units, market-price proxy and carrying assumptions differ. Bitcoin adds BTC-to-USD conversion and a continuous trading calendar; its recorded executions are not SPXW bid–ask midpoints. Changing volatility, jumps, financing and index timing can put pressure on a constant-volatility benchmark. These studies do not isolate which factor caused a particular error.
