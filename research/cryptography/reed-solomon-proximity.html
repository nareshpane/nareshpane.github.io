A Computational Laboratory for Reed–Solomon Proximity Gaps

This page develops Reed–Solomon codes from the ground up and then turns the proximity-gap question into a sequence of explicit experiments. Every stage has a concrete polynomial, a finite field, a reproducible Python script, and a separate output file. The purpose is not to hide the difficulty behind abstract notation, but to let the reader watch the difficulty appear.

Current-status correction, July 11, 2026. The phrase “the Reed–Solomon proximity-gap conjecture” no longer refers to one untouched yes-or-no problem. Proximity gaps were proved in the Johnson-bound regime, while several stronger up-to-capacity conjectures used in proof systems were disproved in late 2025. A 2026 construction also established failures near capacity over prime fields. Modified conjectures and related curve-decoding questions remain active. Therefore, the experiments below are presented as a laboratory for the phenomenon—not as an attempted computational proof of a statement already known to be false in its broadest form.
Core distinction. The worst-case list size $L_t=\max_r |\{c\in C:d_H(r,c)\le t\}|$ is a list-decoding quantity. A proximity gap concerns a structured collection of words—such as an affine line $\{f+zg:z\in\mathbb F_q\}$—and asks whether the fraction of line points close to the code can occupy a large intermediate range. The first quantity is a useful warm-up; it is not the full conjecture.

1. What exactly is being tested?

Choose a finite field $\mathbb F_q$, choose $n$ distinct evaluation points $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$, and choose a degree bound $k$. The ordinary Reed–Solomon code is

$$C=RS(\mathbb F_q,\{\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n\},k) =\{(p(\alpha_1),\ldots,p(\alpha_n)): \deg p<k\}. $$

A message is not a sentence. It is the coefficient vector of a polynomial. For $k=3$, for example, a message has the form $(a_0,a_1,a_2)$ and represents $p(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2$. Since each coefficient has $q$ possibilities, the code contains exactly $q^k$ codewords.

Finite computation

List-decoding experiment

Fix a received word $r$. Count all codewords within Hamming radius $t$. Then maximize that count over every possible $r\in\mathbb F_q^n$.

Proximity-gap experiment

Affine-line experiment

Fix two words $f,g$. Inspect every point $f+zg$ as $z$ runs through the field. Count how many line points are close to $C$, then compare that with a shared polynomial-pair explanation.

These two experiments are connected because a line point can have several nearby polynomial explanations. However, a proximity gap is a statement about the pattern across a whole structured family, not merely about one large list around one received word.

3. Step zero: arithmetic in $\mathbb F_7$

The field $\mathbb F_7$ consists of $0,1,2,3,4,5,6$. Addition and multiplication are performed normally and then reduced modulo $7$. For example,

$$5+6=11\equiv4\pmod 7,\qquad 4\cdot5=20\equiv6\pmod 7.$$

Division means multiplication by an inverse. Since $3\cdot5=15\equiv1\pmod7$, the inverse of $3$ is $5$. This matters in interpolation, where denominators such as $(x_j-x_m)$ must be inverted.

Ordinary modular arithmetic gives a field only when $q$ is prime. Thus arithmetic modulo $7$ is a field, but arithmetic modulo $8$ is not. The later $GF(8)$ experiment constructs an extension field rather than reducing integers modulo $8$.

5. Experiment 2: reconstruct the polynomial by interpolation

A degree-$2$ polynomial is determined by any three distinct points. From the codeword above, keep only $(0,3)$, $(2,2)$, and $(5,1)$. Lagrange interpolation writes

$$p(x)=3L_0(x)+2L_1(x)+1L_2(x),$$ $$L_j(x)=\prod_{m\ne j}\frac{x-x_m}{x_j-x_m}.$$

In coefficient-vector form over $\mathbb F_7$, the three contributions are

PointContribution coefficients $[a_0,a_1,a_2]$
$(0,3)$$[3,0,1]$
$(2,2)$$[0,4,2]$
$(5,1)$$[0,5,1]$

Adding coordinatewise modulo $7$ gives $[3,0,1]+[0,4,2]+[0,5,1]=[3,9,4]\equiv[3,2,4]$, exactly recovering $p(x)=3+2x+4x^2$.

This simple fact drives both the power and the difficulty of Reed–Solomon codes. Any $k$ coordinates determine one candidate degree-$<k$ polynomial. When many different $k$-subsets produce different candidates that all agree with a received word on their chosen coordinates, list sizes can grow combinatorially.

6. Experiment 3: build all $343$ codewords

With $q=7$ and $k=3$, there are $7^3=343$ coefficient triples. Evaluating each polynomial at six points creates the entire $RS(7,6,3)$ codebook. Its minimum distance is $d=n-k+1=4$.

Full enumeration Script 03

Start with the codeword for $p(x)=3+2x+4x^2$:

sent     = [3, 2, 2, 3, 5, 1]
received = [3, 3, 2, 3, 1, 1]  # coordinates 1 and 4 changed

The received word is distance $2$ from the original codeword, but it is also distance $2$ from the codeword generated by $2+2x+6x^2$. Thus there are two nearest polynomial explanations.

This does not contradict error correction. Since $d=4$, the guaranteed unique-decoding radius is only $\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor=1$. At two errors, uniqueness is no longer guaranteed.

9. Experiment 5: a clean interpolation-crowding construction

Let the code contain polynomials of degree less than $k$, but define the received word using the degree-$k$ polynomial $r(x)=x^k$. Choose any subset $S$ of $k$ evaluation coordinates. There is one degree-$<k$ polynomial $p_S$ that interpolates $r$ on those coordinates.

The difference $x^k-p_S(x)$ is a nonzero polynomial of degree $k$ with the $k$ points in $S$ as roots. It cannot have another distinct root. Therefore $p_S$ agrees with $r$ on exactly those $k$ coordinates, and two different subsets give two different polynomials. Hence

$$|\{p:\deg p<k,\ d_H(r,\operatorname{eval}(p))\le n-k\}|=\binom nk.$$

10. From list decoding to affine-line proximity

Now choose two arbitrary words $f,g\in\mathbb F_q^n$ with $g\ne0$. They define the affine line

$$\ell(f,g)=\{f+zg:z\in\mathbb F_q\}.$$

The line has $q$ points. For a symbol-error radius $t$, define the number of close points

$$N_t(f,g)=|\{z\in\mathbb F_q:d_H(f+zg,C)\le t\}|.$$

A proximity-gap statement says, roughly, that a structured set should not have an uncontrolled medium-sized close portion: either every member is close, or only a small fraction is close. The exact thresholds depend on the theorem or conjecture being studied.

Correlated agreement

A stronger structural explanation asks whether one polynomial $P$ explains $f$ and another polynomial $Q$ explains $g$ on many of the same coordinates. Define

$$d_H([f,g],C^2)= \min_{P,Q:\deg P,\deg Q<k} |\{i:(f_i,g_i)\ne(P(\alpha_i),Q(\alpha_i))\}|.$$

If $f$ and $g$ agree with $P$ and $Q$ on a common coordinate set $S$, then every line point $f+zg$ agrees on $S$ with the code polynomial $P+zQ$. This is why correlated agreement can explain an entire line at once.

The converse with zero loss is not automatic. Different values of $z$ may be close to entirely different polynomials, with no single common coordinate set large enough to explain $f$ and $g$ at the same radius.

12. Experiment 7: exact census of all affine lines in $\mathbb F_5^4$

A random search can miss rare behavior, so the smallest line experiment is exhaustive. A nonzero direction has $q-1$ scalar representations, giving

$$\frac{q^n-1}{q-1}=\frac{5^4-1}{4}=156$$ distinct one-dimensional directions.

For each direction, the space splits into $q^{n-1}=5^3=125$ parallel affine lines. Therefore the exact number of distinct affine lines is

$$156\cdot125=19{,}500.$$

This does not contradict an asymptotic theorem that permits an error fraction depending on $n$ and $q$; it simply shows why one must preserve the theorem’s actual quantitative parameters. At radius $2=n-k$, every word in $\mathbb F_5^4$ is at most two errors from some degree-$1$ polynomial: choose any two coordinates and interpolate a line. Thus every affine line is entirely close at the capacity boundary.

14. Experiment 10: why $GF(8)$ is not arithmetic modulo $8$

The number $8=2^3$ is a prime power, so a field with eight elements exists. However, it is not $\mathbb Z/8\mathbb Z$, because $2\cdot4\equiv0\pmod8$ even though neither factor is zero. Instead use

$$GF(8)=\mathbb F_2[a]/(a^3+a+1).$$

Each field element is a binary polynomial $b_0+b_1a+b_2a^2$. Addition is bitwise XOR, and multiplication reduces $a^3$ using $a^3=a+1$. The script evaluates $p(x)=1+ax+a^2x^2$ at all eight field elements and recovers the coefficients from three points.

Why smarter search is still mathematically difficult

Random sampling can find common behavior but may miss highly structured counterexamples. Symmetry reduction removes duplicate lines but not the exponential core. Integer programming or SAT encodings can enforce local agreement constraints, but the number of possible nearby polynomials also grows. Algebraic constructions can bypass brute force, yet then the problem becomes proving that many scalar values remain distinct and that no correlated explanation exists. This is precisely where coding theory, additive combinatorics, and number theory enter.

17. What these computations prove—and what they do not

ObservationLegitimate conclusionIllegitimate leap
The interpolation script finds exactly $\binom nk$ candidates. The stated finite construction has that exact list size; there is also a direct proof. Every worst-case received word must arise from the same construction.
All 19,500 lines in $\mathbb F_5^4$ were checked. The reported census is exact for $q=5,n=4,k=2$ and the chosen radii. The same fractions persist as $n$ grows or under another field-size regime.
A sampled line has all points close but pair distance larger than $t$. Zero-loss correlated agreement fails for that exact finite line and radius. A formal conjecture with proximity loss, error thresholds, or asymptotic quantifiers is refuted.
A random search finds no candidate. No candidate appeared in that reproducible sample. No counterexample exists.
The most productive computational objective is often not “settle the conjecture by brute force.” It is “identify the exact finite mechanism that a theorem must control.” The scripts isolate three such mechanisms: interpolation crowding, changing nearest polynomials along a line, and the explosive number of affine-line parameterizations.

18. Research status and primary references

The 2020 work of Ben-Sasson, Carmon, Ishai, Kopparty, and Saraf proved proximity gaps for Reed–Solomon codes below the Johnson/Guruswami–Sudan bound, with applications to proof systems and distributed protocols. The journal version appeared in 2023.

Crites and Stewart’s November 2025 preprint disproved several stronger up-to-capacity conjectures, including correlated agreement, mutual correlated agreement, and a list-decodability conjecture used in analyses of FRI-family systems. They also proposed modified forms of the conjectures.

Kambiré’s April 2026 report gave an explicit family over prime fields where proximity gaps fail at radii only $O(1/\log n)$ below capacity. Meanwhile, 2026 work on folded Reed–Solomon codes, subspace-design codes, random evaluation points, and curve-decoding continues to map which code families retain strong proximity behavior.

  2. E. Crites and A. Stewart, “On Reed–Solomon Proximity Gaps Conjectures”, IACR ePrint 2025/2046.

A concrete next research sequence

  1. Hold the rate $R=k/n$ approximately fixed while increasing $n$ over prime fields.
  2. For each case, compute $L_{\lfloor J\rfloor}$ and the first integer radius above $J$.
  3. Search affine lines by structured coordinate stitching, not only uniformly at random.
  4. Canonicalize lines to remove scalar and base-point duplicates before enumeration.
  5. Track both $N_t(f,g)$ and $d_H([f,g],C^2)$, including allowed proximity loss.
  6. Separate standard evaluation sets from multiplicative-subgroup evaluation sets.
  7. Compare prime fields with genuine extension fields such as $GF(8)$ and $GF(16)$.
  8. Only after finite patterns stabilize, formulate the exact asymptotic statement being tested.
The central computational lesson is now visible. A Reed–Solomon word is easy to generate, and any $k$ points are easy to interpolate. The hard part is controlling how thousands or millions of different local interpolations can align across a structured family without producing one global correlated explanation. That is the real proximity-gap problem.
