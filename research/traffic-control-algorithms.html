From One Four-Way Intersection to a Coordinated City Network

How does a traffic light decide who moves next, and how can hundreds of intersections coordinate those decisions across a city?

A signal is a constrained control system. It allocates a shared piece of road while balancing safety, queues, waiting time, turning vehicles and people crossing on foot. Its decisions may use a clock, detectors, or information from neighboring intersections. Watch those decisions take shape.

The intersection · 01

What exactly is being controlled?

An approach is the incoming side of an intersection. A lane is a strip of road; a movement is a particular path through the junction. A vehicle arriving from the north can go straight to the south, turn left toward the east, or turn right toward the west.

Letters name the origin approach: N → S is southbound travel. Arrows show paths, not simultaneous permissions.

A movement is not a phase

A movement describes where someone goes. A phase is a timing/control unit associated with one or more signal indications. Here, “N/S through phase” is convenient shorthand for serving the compatible N → S and S → N movements together.

Real controller terminology can split those into separately timed phases running concurrently; a complete compatible combination is often called a stage. The page uses the simpler combined-phase convention throughout.

Permission is constrained: two paths that occupy the same conflict area cannot both receive protected right of way.

Terminology: FHWA timing manual, chapter 5.

Signal group
A set of signal heads controlled together for a movement or group of movements.
Cycle
One repetition of a signal sequence; fully actuated operation need not repeat at a fixed period.
Green interval
The period granting the relevant movement permission, subject to its indication and yielding rules.
Yellow interval
Notice that the green permission is ending.
All-red interval
Here, a period with both vehicle groups red before a conflicting group receives green.
Cycle length, C
Time between equivalent reference points in successive cycles.
Split
The cycle time allocated to a phase. Engineering splits commonly include its clearance intervals; “green share” below means green alone.
Offset, φ
The time displacement between defined cycle reference points at coordinated intersections.
Detector call
A request for service inferred from a detected vehicle or registered pedestrian demand.
Safety as a graph · 02

Some paths cannot coexist

Let M = {NT, ST, ET, WT, NL, SL, EL, WL}. T means through; L means protected left turn. Define Cij = 1 when two movements conflict, and 0 when this teaching model allows them together.

1 = conflict · 0 = compatible · diagonal = same movement. Hover, focus or click a cell.

Feasible phases as independent sets

A conflict graph has one node per movement and an edge for each conflicting pair. An independent set contains no connected pair: all its movements may coexist under the model. NT and ST form one such set.

Finding compatible sets helps construct a phase plan. It does not set green durations, decide who waits, or specify safe transitions. Real signal engineering is more than graph coloring.

Explicit assumptions. Right-hand traffic; separate approach turn lanes; no permissive turns, right turns or pedestrians in this matrix. Opposing throughs and opposing protected lefts are compatible; a through and left from the same approach are compatible. All other distinct pairs are conservatively excluded, including shared receiving-lane merges. This binary model includes lane conflicts as well as crossing conflicts. Geometry, swept vehicle width and local rules must be checked for a real junction.
A safe sequence · 03

The signal is a finite-state machine

The controller does not jump from one conflicting green to another. It changes through explicit states with timing guards. Select a state to inspect its meaning; this diagram is independent of the opening demonstration and advances once every three seconds for readability, rather than prescribing field timings.

st = (phase, elapsed time, qN, qS, qE, qW, detector calls, pedestrian calls)
at = π(st)  ;  st+1 = f(st, at, arrivalst)

st is what the controller currently knows. The policy π maps that information to an action at, such as hold or request the next phase. The transition rule f advances the state as time passes, vehicles arrive and vehicles depart. A safety layer admits only legal actions.

Time is a safety constraint · 04

Why yellow and all-red exist

When green ends, a driver needs time to perceive the change and react. Some vehicles can stop comfortably; others are already too close. The dilemma zone describes conditions in which neither comfortable stopping nor clearing before the relevant deadline is straightforward. It depends on speed, distance and driver behavior.

dstop ≈ vτ + v² / (2a)  ;  Yillustrative ≈ τ + v / (2a)

v = approach speed in m/s; τ = reaction time; a = assumed comfortable deceleration. The diagram fixes τ = 1 s and a = 3 m/s² on a level approach. Y is a simplified kinematic yellow-time relationship, not a universal specification.

All-red gives vehicles already admitted time to leave the conflict area before a conflicting green. The needed clearance depends on crossing width, vehicle length, speed and the applicable rules. The opening animation demonstrates this explicitly: vehicles enter on green, finish crossing during the transition, then the next group starts.

Illustrative physics only. For engineering context and limitations, see FHWA chapter 4 and the MUTCD; applicable local standards govern actual settings.

The simplest policy · 05

Fixed-time control: reserve the time in advance

repeat forever:
    NS green for G_NS seconds
    NS yellow for 4 seconds
    all red for 2 seconds
    EW green for G_EW seconds
    EW yellow for 4 seconds
    all red for 2 seconds

For GNS = 32 s and GEW = 24 s, the cycle is 68 s. The order and durations repeat whether the approaches are busy or empty.

Demand versus service · 06

A green light has finite capacity

Flow q is vehicles per unit time. Saturation flow s is the discharge rate of a sustained queue under prevailing conditions while it is being served. A movement only receives that service for part of the cycle.

c ≈ s(g / C)

c = approximate capacity in vehicles/hour; s = saturation flow in vehicles/hour of effective green; g = effective green in seconds; C = cycle length in seconds. Effective green accounts for lost time rather than simply copying the displayed green.

The same road, more arrivals

s = 1,800 veh/h and C = 60 s. μ = c is cycle-averaged service. The graph smooths away red intervals and starts with 8 queued vehicles. Equality leaves no spare capacity to absorb fluctuations.

When λ < μ, a persistent queue can dissipate under these stationary assumptions. When λ > μ, the excess accumulates. Doubling demand can cross that threshold even though the signal itself is functioning correctly.

Capacity and lost-time context: FHWA chapter 3.

Conservation of vehicles · 07

A queue is a dynamic system

Qi(t+1) = max{0, Qi(t) + Ai(t) − Di(t)}

Start with the vehicles already present, Q. Add the arrivals, A. Subtract available departures, D. The maximum with zero prevents a negative queue. If D means actual departures, it must already be bounded by Q + A; in this calculator it means potential service.

max(0, 8 + 3 − 5) = 6 vehicles

Listen to the street · 08

Actuated signals respond to calls

A loop detector, camera zone or other sensor can register demand. That observation becomes a call, not an unconditional green. The controller still respects minimum green and safe transitions.

No vehicle in detector
Minimum green
A lower bound on green duration before termination is allowed.
Passage interval
A permitted gap between successive vehicle detections that can keep service active.
Gap-out
Ending a phase when the qualifying arrival gap exceeds the passage setting.
Maximum green
An upper bound that prevents continuing demand from holding green indefinitely under the relevant logic.
Phase call
Registered demand for a movement awaiting its turn.
Pedestrian call
A request whose service must include suitable WALK and clearance timing.
if elapsed < minimum_green:
    hold green
else if vehicles keep arriving and elapsed < maximum_green:
    extend green
else:
    terminate through yellow and all-red
    consider the next requested phase

Identical uneven arrivals, two policies

Same scheduled arrivals: a 20-second N/S burst each minute and sparse E/W arrivals. Fixed: 24 s green each direction. Actuated: minimum 8 s, 3 s gap, maximum 28 s; opposing demand required to switch. Both use 3 s yellow + 2 s all-red and 1 vehicle/2 s service. This particular demand trace is an example, not a benchmark.

Actuation can avoid serving an empty approach. Fixed timing can be predictable for a corridor, and detector failure or poor settings can undermine actuation. Neither policy always wins.

A choice, with constraints · 09

Who should go next?

A teaching policy can assign a score to each candidate phase. This is a way to expose tradeoffs, not a formula that every city controller computes.

scorei = wqQi + wwWi + wpPi

Q = queue count; W = oldest wait in tens of seconds; P = pedestrian-call indicator (0 or 1). The weights convert these different measures into a dimensionless teaching score.

CandidateQOldest waitPScore
N/S through1420 s0
E/W + compatible crossing690 s1

Always serving the longest queue can starve a quiet approach. A maximum-wait guard can override the score at a safe decision point; it cannot bypass an active clearance interval. A pedestrian request also requires a feasible crossing duration and compatible turning restrictions.

Look downstream · 10

Pressure is more than a long queue

Serving a queue helps only if vehicles have somewhere to go. A simple pressure is the difference between the upstream queue Qi and downstream queue Qj.

pij = Qi − Qj
P(phase) = Σactive (i,j) pij  ;  phase* = arg maxfeasible phases P(phase)

Route B stays at 10 upstream and 9 downstream (pressure 1). A downstream queue of 20 fills the assumed storage, making that release infeasible even when its raw score is attractive.

In max-pressure research, pressure generally uses movement queues, routing proportions and service-rate weights. The formula above strips those details away. Stability results depend on model assumptions; they do not guarantee short waits or prevent physical spillback in every road network. Finite storage needs explicit attention. More users, more constraints · 11

Pedestrians and left turns change the plan

A protected left receives a dedicated indication with conflicting traffic held. A permissive left relies on yielding under the applicable rules. A pedestrian WALK starts a crossing opportunity; the subsequent clearance indication allows people already crossing to finish. It is not an invitation to start a new crossing.

Protected N/S left turns

WALK →Clearance / flashing DON'T WALK →DON'T WALK

Each vehicle-phase transition also needs its own clearance. Right turns across active crossings are prohibited in this illustration. Elsewhere they may be permitted with yielding, depending on local rules.

This four-stage plan is one example. Different layouts use different phasing, turn restrictions, overlaps and pedestrian intervals. The opening demonstration uses an exclusive pedestrian stage to make compatibility particularly clear. [Signal indications]

From one intersection to a corridor · 12

Now add a second intersection

A green at A releases a platoon: a group of vehicles traveling together. B can use a common cycle and a suitable offset to meet the group with green. It needs a timing relationship with A, not direct communication with every signal in the city.

40 km/h = 11.1 m/s  ;  ttravel ≈ 400 / 11.1 ≈ 36 s
φ ≈ ttravel mod C

φ is B's green-start displacement relative to A in this example; “mod C” means wrap around the cycle. A different cycle reference, queue at B or platoon release profile changes the useful offset.

Two copies of the same eight-vehicle release. C = 80 s, B green lasts 22 s. Upper row: offset 36 s. Lower row: offset 0 s; arrivals then meet red and queue. Vehicle spacing and acceleration are schematic.

Time becomes a second map · 13

The green wave

Five intersections can offer a moving window of permission. In a time–space diagram, horizontal position is time and vertical position is distance along the road. A constant-speed vehicle draws a diagonal line. It can progress without stopping only where that line meets a green band at every intersection.

A corridor, seen in two coordinate systems

Interactive

400 m spacing, 28 s green at each signal, no turns or side-street vehicles in the animation. Diagonal blue lines are desired unimpeded trajectories; a red intersection on a line predicts a stop. The moving road vehicles obey the signals and queue. The gold diagonal band shows feasible departures from signal 1, when one exists.

Progression bandwidth is the width, in departure time, of an uninterrupted band through the green windows at the assumed speed. It is an opportunity for progression, not a guarantee that all drivers travel without delay. [Coordination]

There is no universal green wave · 14

The opposite direction has its own clock

With an 80-second cycle and 36-second travel time, eastbound progression asks for φB − φA ≈ 36 s. Westbound asks for the opposite difference, equivalent to 44 s. Finite green windows may accommodate both partially, but their ideal reference points differ.

Side streets need service. Pedestrians need crossing time. Buses dwell at stops; vehicles turn; arrivals spread out; intersections have different demands. A queue can occupy the space that a green wave expected to be empty. Citywide coordination therefore balances several objectives under limited road capacity. “Make every light green” does not resolve those conflicts.

The city as a dynamic graph · 15

Now the state lives on road links

G = (V, E)
x(t+1) = F(x(t), u(t), d(t))

V is the set of intersections; E is the set of directed road links. xe(t) records vehicles or queue state on link e. uv(t) is the phase selected at intersection v.

The vector x gathers all link states; u gathers all control decisions; d contains demand entering from outside the network. F describes arrivals, transfers, discharge and storage constraints.

J = Σt [α Σi Qi(t) + β Σi delayi(t) + γ Σi stopsi(t)]

The controller might minimize J: queues, delay and stopping receive policy weights α, β and γ. Their units and normalization must be chosen deliberately; these measures overlap and should not be interpreted as independent costs automatically.

Any candidate remains subject to safety, feasible phase transitions, minimum green, vehicle clearance, pedestrian timing and network capacity. The shortest mathematical expression often hides the longest engineering specification.

Where decisions happen · 16

Centralized, distributed, or both?

A central system can coordinate objectives over a wide area but relies on communications, reliable observations and scalable computation. Local decisions can respond quickly but may overlook effects several links away. Hybrid designs combine regional plans with local actuation; actual architectures vary.

Established systems, distinct mechanisms · 17

Adaptive control changes the plan as demand changes

Adaptive control uses measured traffic to update timing decisions. It need not use machine learning. A system may alter cycle length, splits or offsets, with local controllers continuing to enforce the permitted signal sequence.

Deployed system

SCOOT

The Split Cycle Offset Optimisation Technique uses detector observations and a traffic model to support repeated timing adjustments. Its name identifies the three principal timing quantities. TRL documents SCOOT as the optimization component of its urban traffic control offering.

TRL's SCOOT / UTC description

Deployed system

SCATS

The Sydney Coordinated Adaptive Traffic System is a Transport for NSW system. Detector volume and non-occupancy measurements inform adjustments to cycle lengths, splits and offsets. Its documented architecture combines a central management layer, regional coordination and local controller software. Degree of saturation is one of its traffic-measurement concepts.

Transport for NSW's SCATS materials

The distinction matters. The lab below implements neither SCOOT nor SCATS. Its “actuated” and “pressure-based” modes are small, transparent teaching policies. Max-pressure, MPC and RL are research families with many variants, not interchangeable names for those commercial/deployed systems.

Capabilities depend on controller generation, detector configuration, communications, local policy and deployment. This page makes no city-specific claim about which algorithm is currently running.

Optimize, act once, reobserve · 18

Model predictive control looks a few steps ahead

Estimate the current state. Predict possible futures. Evaluate candidate signal decisions. Choose a low-cost feasible sequence, execute only its first action, and measure again. This repeated planning is model predictive control (MPC).

minut, …, ut+H−1 Σk=0H−1 L(xt+k, ut+k)

H is the prediction horizon; k indexes future steps; L scores each predicted state and action. Predictions must obey traffic dynamics and signal constraints. Only the first planned action is committed.

A moving horizon

A conceptual five-step plan. Each rectangle is a 10-second decision block; a change consumes 5 seconds of clearance. The displayed candidate is recalculated by enumerating all 32 hold/switch sequences in a two-queue teaching model. Forecast demand changes with the horizon; it is not real sensor data.

A longer horizon can anticipate an arriving platoon, but it increases computation and exposes the plan to forecast error. A model must also describe storage and discharge well enough for its predicted advantage to be meaningful. [MPC research]

Learning a policy · 19

Reinforcement learning changes how the policy is obtained

State

Queues, active phase, its duration and detector occupancy describe what the agent observes.

Action

Hold, or request another feasible phase. A safety supervisor still enforces allowed transitions.

Reward

A numerical signal evaluates the result after traffic and the controller transition to a new state.

rt = −Σi Qi(t)  ;  π(a | s)

The minimal reward r penalizes queued vehicles. A learned policy π(a | s) assigns probabilities to actions given a state; an action-value function Q(s,a) instead estimates future return. This Q(s,a) is a learning quantity, distinct from the queue count Qi.

Observe state → choose a permitted action → traffic changes → receive reward → update a policy during training. The page's city lab contains no trained RL agent.

The appeal is learning decisions in a complicated interacting network. The obstacles include simulation-to-real mismatch, unsafe exploration, rare events, failed detectors, changing demand, interpretability and coordination among agents. Reward design can also hide inequity: small total queues may coexist with an unacceptably long wait for one user. RL is not automatically superior to calibrated classical control. [Deployment challenges]

When storage becomes the constraint · 20

Green does not create downstream space

A full receiving link makes further discharge useless or harmful. Its queue can extend upstream—spillback—and obstruct crossing movements. If several links block one another, the network can become gridlocked.

A conceptual storage demonstration, not a driver-behavior model. Without gating, excess entries occupy the crossing and block its perpendicular flow. With gating, they remain upstream while downstream departures create space.

Queue detection, gating entry into congested regions and preserving downstream storage can keep a blockage from spreading. These redistribute delay; they cannot eliminate demand that exceeds sustainable capacity. Simple pressure differences alone do not encode every physical storage restriction. [Finite-capacity limitation]

Different requests, different authority · 21

A bus request is not emergency preemption

Coordination

Schedules regular service so platoons may progress along a corridor. It need not give preference to any individual vehicle.

Transit priority

A qualifying bus might receive a green extension or an earlier green. Eligibility may depend on lateness, occupancy, route policy and available infrastructure.

Emergency preemption

A higher-priority request can interrupt normal sequencing under defined transition and recovery rules. It is a stronger intervention than ordinary priority.

Neither mechanism means conflicting users instantly lose all clearance time. Local requirements govern transition, pedestrian handling and recovery; coordination may take time to reestablish. [FHWA signal program handbook]

Read the model before comparing numbers

This is a discrete store-and-forward network with one incoming queue for each of two directions at each node: southbound from the north and eastbound from the west. There are no reverse directions, turns, lane changes or pedestrian calls. Each receiving approach holds at most 20 vehicles, including vehicles in transit to it. Transfers take 4 seconds and reserve storage. Excess boundary demand waits outside the network rather than disappearing.

Time advances in deterministic 1-second steps. Each green can serve one vehicle every 2 seconds. All strategies include 3 s yellow and 2 s all-red. Fixed timing divides the remaining cycle equally and applies an offset of (row + column) × the selected value. Actuated operation uses 8 s minimum, 28 s maximum and a 3 s arrival gap. Pressure-based operation compares upstream minus downstream queues after 8 s and forces a pending other direction after 28 s. Cycle and offset controls apply only to fixed-time operation.

Mean queue is the current total queued divided by 18 approaches. Waiting time is the average time from joining an approach queue to its departure, across completed queue visits; vehicles still waiting and external backlog are excluded, so inspect queues alongside it. A stop episode is counted when a vehicle joins a red or nonempty queue. Throughput counts boundary exits since restart. Metrics are teaching proxies, not calibrated travel-time or emissions estimates.

Audit one decision · 24

One complete numerical example

Initially QNS = 15 and QEW = 7. The next interval brings ANS = 3 and AEW = 5. Green permits at most 8 departures for N/S or 7 for E/W. Assume either candidate can be served safely within the modeled interval; any transition loss is already included in those service estimates.

Serve N/S

Q′NS = 15 + 3 − 8 = 10
Q′EW = 7 + 5 − 0 = 12
Total = 22 vehicles

No queue reaches the zero bound.

Serve E/W

Q′NS = 15 + 3 − 0 = 18
Q′EW = 7 + 5 − 7 = 5
Total = 23 vehicles

One fewer departure leaves one more vehicle.

Minimizing next-interval total queue chooses N/S: 22 < 23. The preference is only one vehicle. A fairness constraint, a downstream blockage, pedestrian service or a longer prediction horizon could change the decision. A longest-queue rule happens to agree here; that coincidence is not a general proof.

Keep the mathematics attached to the street

Six equations to take away

Conserve vehicles

Qi(t+1) = max{0, Qi(t) + Ai(t) − Di(t)}

The next queue equals the current queue plus arrivals minus potential service, bounded below by zero.

Select an action

at = π(st)

A policy π maps observed state s to action a. Safety constraints restrict what that action may do.

Propagate the network

x(t+1) = F(x(t), u(t), d(t))

F advances all road states x under signal decisions u and external demand d.

Allocate capacity

ci ≈ si gi / C

Saturation flow s is discounted by effective green g as a fraction of cycle length C.

Look downstream

pij = Qi − Qj

Simplified pressure compares upstream and downstream queues. It does not replace a storage constraint.

State the objective

min Σt Σi [αQi(t) + β delayi(t) + γ stopsi(t)]

Weights α, β and γ express a choice about costs. Minimize only over safe, feasible signal sequences.
