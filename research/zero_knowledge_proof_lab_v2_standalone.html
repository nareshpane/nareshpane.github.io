1. The big idea Back to top

A zero-knowledge proof separates two tasks that normally seem inseparable: proving that a claim is true and revealing the secret that makes it true. A prover may demonstrate knowledge of a password, a graph colouring, a hidden exponent, or a valid computation while keeping the witness private.

$$\text{Convince the verifier that a statement is true}\quad\text{without disclosing the witness.}$$

Prover

Knows a secret witness and constructs the proof.

Verifier

Checks the proof using only public information and the protocol transcript.

Adversary

May try to convince the verifier without possessing a valid witness.

Zero knowledge does not mean that the verifier learns literally nothing. The verifier learns that the public statement is valid, or at least becomes highly confident in its validity. The “zero” refers to additional knowledge about the hidden witness.

2. Statements, witnesses, and relations Back to top

Cryptographers describe the claim using a public statement $x$, a private witness $w$, and a relation $R$. The witness is valid when

3. The three properties Back to top

Completeness

If the statement is true and both parties follow the protocol, the verifier accepts with probability $1$ or overwhelmingly close to $1$.

Soundness

If the statement is false, a cheating prover should be accepted only with small probability. Repetition can often reduce this error.

Zero knowledge

The verifier’s view can be reproduced without the witness, meaning the transcript reveals no useful witness information beyond validity.

Type of zero knowledgeMeaning of “real view and simulated view are alike”
PerfectThe two distributions are exactly identical.
StatisticalThe distributions may differ, but only by a negligibly small statistical distance.
ComputationalNo efficient algorithm can distinguish the distributions with meaningful advantage.

4. Why simulation is the heart of zero knowledge Back to top

A simulator is an algorithm that does not know the secret witness but can nevertheless generate a transcript that looks like a real verifier’s view. If the verifier can manufacture an equivalent-looking transcript without the witness, then the real conversation could not have supplied useful witness information.

Schnorr simulator for an honest verifier. Choose a challenge $c$ and response $s$ first, then compute $$t=g^s y^{-c}\pmod p.$$ The resulting tuple $(t,c,s)$ satisfies $g^s=t y^c$. It looks like an accepting transcript even though the simulator never used $x$.

This does not let a live cheater defeat the protocol, because a live cheater must publish $t$ before learning the verifier’s unpredictable challenge. Simulation is allowed to choose the challenge first because it is a mathematical experiment used to analyze what information the transcript contains.

5. Commitments: sealed digital envelopes Back to top

A commitment scheme has two core goals:

Hiding

Before opening, the receiver should not learn the committed value.

Binding

After committing, the sender should not be able to open the same commitment as two different values.

A simple hash-based teaching example is $C=H( ho\Vert m)$, where $m$ is the value and $ ho$ is random salt. Real commitment schemes require careful definitions, encoding rules, and security assumptions; a short hash expression should not automatically be treated as a complete production protocol.

7. Experiment 1: Ali Baba cave: soundness before algebra Back to top

Experiment 1 Level 1 · intuition and probability Interactive story

Question

Can repeated random challenges make a lie extremely unlikely, while the secret itself remains hidden?

Intuition

The prover enters one of two cave passages. A secret door connects the passages, but it opens only for someone who knows the password. After the prover is hidden, the verifier randomly requests an exit. An honest prover can use the secret door and always emerge from the requested side. A cheater can only hope that the requested side matches the side originally chosen.

Mathematical core

For one round, a cheater succeeds with probability $1/2$. Independent repetition multiplies probabilities, so after $t$ rounds, $$\Pr[\text{cheater accepted}] = \left(\frac12\right)^t = 2^{-t}.$$ The honest prover's acceptance probability is $1$.

Concrete walkthrough

At $t=10$, the theoretical cheating probability is $2^{-10}=1/1024\approx0.0009766$. The simulation runs thousands of cheating attempts and measures the fraction that survive all ten challenges. The empirical result should fluctuate near the theoretical value.

What you should learn

  • Completeness: the honest prover always succeeds.
  • Soundness error: a cheater may pass, but only with a quantifiable small probability.
  • Amplification: repeating the protocol reduces soundness error exponentially.

Experiments to try

  • Change `MAX_ROUNDS` from 12 to 20 and observe the logarithmic plot.
  • Reduce `TRIALS` to see how noisy empirical probability estimates become.
  • Replace the two paths by three possible doors; predict the new cheating probability before coding it.
Self-check questions.
  1. Why must the verifier choose the requested exit only after the prover has entered?
  2. Why does one successful round provide weak evidence, while twenty rounds provide strong evidence?
  3. What information does the verifier learn besides “the prover probably knows the password”?

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
01_ali_baba_cave.py
A first experiment on zero-knowledge proofs: the Ali Baba cave.
Educational only.
"""

from __future__ import annotations
import math
import random
from pathlib import Path

try:
    import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
except Exception:
    plt = None

RNG = random.Random(42)
TRIALS = 20000
MAX_ROUNDS = 12
IMAGE_PATH = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[1] / 'images' / '01_ali_baba_cave_probability.png'


def one_trial(rounds: int, honest: bool, rng: random.Random) -> bool:
    """Return True if the prover is accepted for all rounds."""
    for _ in range(rounds):
        chosen_path = rng.choice(['A', 'B'])
        verifier_request = rng.choice(['A', 'B'])
        if honest:
            success = True  # honest prover can switch paths using the secret word
        else:
            success = (chosen_path == verifier_request)
        if not success:
            return False
    return True


def estimate(rounds: int, honest: bool, trials: int, rng: random.Random) -> float:
    wins = sum(one_trial(rounds, honest, rng) for _ in range(trials))
    return wins / trials


def main() -> None:
    print('ALI BABA CAVE: EMPIRICAL SOUNDNESS TEST')
    print('=' * 60)
    print('A cheating prover has success probability (1/2)^t after t rounds.')
    print()
    print(f"{'Rounds':>6}  {'Theory':>12}  {'Empirical':>12}  {'Honest':>12}")
    print('-' * 50)

    xs, theory_vals, empirical_vals = [], [], []
    for rounds in range(1, MAX_ROUNDS + 1):
        theory = 2 ** (-rounds)
        empirical = estimate(rounds, honest=False, trials=TRIALS, rng=RNG)
        honest = estimate(rounds, honest=True, trials=2000, rng=RNG)
        xs.append(rounds)
        theory_vals.append(theory)
        empirical_vals.append(empirical)
        print(f"{rounds:6d}  {theory:12.8f}  {empirical:12.8f}  {honest:12.8f}")

    print()
    print('Interpretation:')
    print('- The honest prover always passes.')
    print('- The cheating prover quickly becomes extremely unlikely to pass many rounds.')
    print('- This illustrates soundness without revealing the secret word.')

    if plt is not None:
        plt.figure(figsize=(8, 5))
        plt.plot(xs, theory_vals, marker='o', label='Theory: $2^{-t}$')
        plt.plot(xs, empirical_vals, marker='s', label='Empirical cheating rate')
        plt.yscale('log')
        plt.xlabel('Number of rounds t')
        plt.ylabel('Cheating acceptance probability')
        plt.title('Ali Baba Cave: cheating probability falls exponentially')
        plt.grid(True, which='both', alpha=0.3)
        plt.legend()
        plt.tight_layout()
        plt.savefig(IMAGE_PATH, dpi=180)
        print(f"\nSaved figure to: {IMAGE_PATH}")
    else:
        print('\nmatplotlib not available, so no PNG was created.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

8. Experiment 2: Graph isomorphism: prove a hidden relabeling Back to top

Experiment 2 Level 2 · permutations and simulation Combinatorial ZKP

Question

Can the prover demonstrate that two graphs are the same up to relabeling without revealing the relabeling?

Intuition

The public statement is that $G_0$ and $G_1$ are isomorphic. The witness is a secret permutation $\sigma$ satisfying $G_1=\sigma(G_0)$. The prover randomizes $G_0$ again before the verifier asks which public graph must be mapped to the randomized graph.

Mathematical core

Choose a random permutation $\tau$ and send $$H=\tau(G_0).$$ The verifier sends a random bit $b\in\{0,1\}$. The prover responds with $$\phi=\begin{cases} \tau, & b=0,\\ \tau\circ\sigma^{-1}, & b=1. \end{cases}$$ The verifier checks $\phi(G_b)=H$. A cheater prepared for only one challenge succeeds with probability at most $1/2$ per round.

Concrete walkthrough

Suppose $G_1=\sigma(G_0)$. For challenge $b=1$, $$(\tau\circ\sigma^{-1})(G_1) =(\tau\circ\sigma^{-1})(\sigma(G_0)) =\tau(G_0)=H.$$ The important zero-knowledge idea is simulation: a simulator may first choose $b$ and a random $\phi$, then set $H=\phi(G_b)$. The resulting transcript has the same form as a real accepting transcript, despite the simulator not knowing $\sigma$.

What you should learn

  • A witness can be a combinatorial object such as a permutation.
  • Randomization hides which original labels correspond to which randomized labels.
  • Simulation is the mathematical language used to express “the verifier learned nothing extra.”

Experiments to try

  • Change the five-vertex graph and choose a new secret permutation.
  • Create a non-isomorphic graph pair and verify that brute-force isomorphism testing returns `False`.
  • Increase the number of rounds and compare the cheating rate to $2^{-t}$.
Self-check questions.
  1. Why does revealing a fresh random permutation not reveal the fixed secret permutation?
  2. Why can a prover who knows the isomorphism answer either challenge?
  3. How does the simulator generate an accepting transcript without the witness?

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
02_graph_isomorphism_zkp.py
Classic zero-knowledge proof for graph isomorphism.
Educational only; graphs are tiny so brute-force checks are possible.
"""

from __future__ import annotations
import itertools
import random
from typing import Iterable

RNG = random.Random(123)


def normalize_edge(u: int, v: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    return (u, v) if u < v else (v, u)


def apply_perm(graph: set[tuple[int, int]], perm: list[int]) -> set[tuple[int, int]]:
    return {normalize_edge(perm[u], perm[v]) for (u, v) in graph}


def compose(p: list[int], q: list[int]) -> list[int]:
    """Return p∘q, meaning apply q first, then p."""
    return [p[q[i]] for i in range(len(p))]


def inverse(perm: list[int]) -> list[int]:
    inv = [0] * len(perm)
    for i, j in enumerate(perm):
        inv[j] = i
    return inv


def is_isomorphic(g1: set[tuple[int, int]], g2: set[tuple[int, int]], n: int) -> bool:
    for perm in itertools.permutations(range(n)):
        if apply_perm(g1, list(perm)) == g2:
            return True
    return False


def honest_round(g0, g1, sigma, rng):
    n = max(max(e) for e in g0) + 1
    tau = list(range(n))
    rng.shuffle(tau)
    H = apply_perm(g0, tau)
    b = rng.randint(0, 1)
    if b == 0:
        phi = tau
    else:
        phi = compose(tau, inverse(sigma))
    verified = (apply_perm(g0 if b == 0 else g1, phi) == H)
    return b, verified


def cheating_round(noniso_g0, noniso_g1, rng):
    """A bluffing prover can only prepare to answer one challenge."""
    n = max(max(e) for e in noniso_g0) + 1
    choice = rng.randint(0, 1)
    base = noniso_g0 if choice == 0 else noniso_g1
    tau = list(range(n))
    rng.shuffle(tau)
    H = apply_perm(base, tau)
    b = rng.randint(0, 1)
    if b == choice:
        phi = tau
        verified = (apply_perm(noniso_g0 if b == 0 else noniso_g1, phi) == H)
    else:
        verified = False
    return b, verified


def multi_round(round_fn, rounds: int, trials: int) -> float:
    wins = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        ok = True
        for _ in range(rounds):
            _, verified = round_fn()
            if not verified:
                ok = False
                break
        wins += ok
    return wins / trials


def main() -> None:
    # Isomorphic pair
    g0 = {normalize_edge(*e) for e in [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4), (1, 4)]}
    sigma = [2, 4, 0, 1, 3]
    g1 = apply_perm(g0, sigma)

    # Non-isomorphic pair for cheating demo
    h0 = {normalize_edge(*e) for e in [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4)]}   # path
    h1 = {normalize_edge(*e) for e in [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 0), (3, 4)]}   # triangle + edge

    print('GRAPH ISOMORPHISM ZERO-KNOWLEDGE PROOF')
    print('=' * 60)
    print('We prove knowledge of a hidden permutation sigma such that G1 = sigma(G0).')
    print('Protocol equations are combinatorial rather than algebraic:')
    print('  H = tau(G0)')
    print('  if challenge b=0, reveal phi = tau')
    print('  if challenge b=1, reveal phi = tau ∘ sigma^{-1}')
    print()
    print('Sanity checks:')
    print('  G0 and G1 isomorphic?   ', is_isomorphic(g0, g1, 5))
    print('  H0 and H1 isomorphic?   ', is_isomorphic(h0, h1, 5))
    print()

    b, verified = honest_round(g0, g1, sigma, RNG)
    print(f'Example honest round challenge b = {b}, verifier accepts = {verified}')
    print()
    print(f"{'Rounds':>6}  {'Honest accept':>14}  {'Cheater accept':>15}  {'Theory cheater':>15}")
    print('-' * 60)
    for rounds in range(1, 8):
        honest = multi_round(lambda: honest_round(g0, g1, sigma, RNG), rounds, 2000)
        cheat = multi_round(lambda: cheating_round(h0, h1, RNG), rounds, 5000)
        theory = 2 ** (-rounds)
        print(f"{rounds:6d}  {honest:14.6f}  {cheat:15.6f}  {theory:15.6f}")

    print('\nInterpretation: a prover who does not know how to answer both challenges can do no better than about 1/2 per round.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

9. Experiment 3: Three-colouring: commitments and selective opening Back to top

Experiment 3 Level 3 · hashes, hiding, and local checks NP-style proof

Question

Can someone prove that a graph has a valid 3-colouring while revealing only the colours on one randomly selected edge?

Intuition

The prover randomly renames the three colours each round, commits to every renamed colour, and lets the verifier select one edge. Only the two endpoint commitments are opened. The verifier checks that the openings are genuine and that the endpoint colours differ.

Mathematical core

For each vertex $v$, choose fresh random salt $\rho_v$ and publish a toy commitment $$\operatorname{Com}(v)=H(\rho_v\,\Vert\,\pi(\operatorname{colour}(v))).$$ If a fake colouring has $B$ bad edges among $m$ total edges and the verifier samples uniformly, then one-round success is $$1-\frac{B}{m},$$ and $t$ independent rounds give $\left(1-B/m\right)^t$. In the supplied fake colouring, two of the five edges are bad, so the prediction is $(3/5)^t$.

Concrete walkthrough

The hash acts as a sealed envelope: after committing, the prover should not be able to change a colour without changing the hash. The random salt prevents a verifier from simply hashing the three possible colour names and comparing them. Because the colour names are freshly permuted every round, seeing “red” on one round does not identify the same underlying colour on another round.

What you should learn

  • Commitments aim to provide both hiding and binding.
  • A verifier can inspect a tiny random portion of a large witness.
  • Fresh random permutations prevent information from accumulating across rounds.

Experiments to try

  • Edit the fake colouring so that exactly one edge is bad; predict the acceptance curve.
  • Add vertices and edges while retaining a known proper colouring.
  • Replace SHA-256 with another hash and confirm that the protocol logic is unchanged.
Self-check questions.
  1. Why is the salt needed when there are only three possible colours?
  2. What does binding prevent the prover from doing after the verifier chooses an edge?
  3. Why is this script educational rather than a production commitment scheme?

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
03_graph_3color_zkp.py
A toy zero-knowledge proof for 3-colourability using hash commitments.
This is a didactic simulation, not production cryptography.
"""

from __future__ import annotations
import hashlib
import os
import random

RNG = random.Random(99)
COLORS = ['red', 'green', 'blue']


def commit(value: str, salt: bytes) -> str:
    return hashlib.sha256(salt + value.encode()).hexdigest()


def permute_coloring(coloring: dict[int, str], rng: random.Random) -> dict[int, str]:
    perm = COLORS[:]
    rng.shuffle(perm)
    mapping = dict(zip(COLORS, perm))
    return {v: mapping[c] for v, c in coloring.items()}


def make_commitments(coloring: dict[int, str]) -> tuple[dict[int, str], dict[int, bytes]]:
    commitments = {}
    salts = {}
    for v, c in coloring.items():
        salt = os.urandom(16)
        salts[v] = salt
        commitments[v] = commit(c, salt)
    return commitments, salts


def verify_opening(vertex: int, claimed_color: str, salt: bytes, commitments: dict[int, str]) -> bool:
    return commit(claimed_color, salt) == commitments[vertex]


def one_round(graph_edges, coloring, rng, honest=True) -> bool:
    if honest:
        shown_coloring = permute_coloring(coloring, rng)
    else:
        shown_coloring = coloring.copy()  # fake prover uses a fixed possibly invalid coloring
    commitments, salts = make_commitments(shown_coloring)
    edge = rng.choice(graph_edges)
    u, v = edge
    cu, cv = shown_coloring[u], shown_coloring[v]
    opened_ok = (
        verify_opening(u, cu, salts[u], commitments)
        and verify_opening(v, cv, salts[v], commitments)
    )
    different = (cu != cv)
    return opened_ok and different


def experiment(graph_edges, honest_coloring, fake_coloring, rounds_max=10, trials=5000):
    print('3-COLOURING ZERO-KNOWLEDGE PROOF')
    print('=' * 60)
    print('Commitment form:')
    print('  Com(v) = H(salt_v || permuted_colour(v))')
    print('The verifier asks to open one random edge (u,v).')
    print('The prover reveals only the colours on that edge, not the full colouring.')
    print()
    print('Graph edges:', graph_edges)
    print('Honest coloring:', honest_coloring)
    print('Fake coloring:  ', fake_coloring)
    print()
    print(f"{'Rounds':>6}  {'Honest accept':>14}  {'Fake accept':>14}")
    print('-' * 40)
    for rounds in range(1, rounds_max + 1):
        honest_ok = 0
        fake_ok = 0
        for _ in range(trials):
            if all(one_round(graph_edges, honest_coloring, RNG, honest=True) for _ in range(rounds)):
                honest_ok += 1
            if all(one_round(graph_edges, fake_coloring, RNG, honest=False) for _ in range(rounds)):
                fake_ok += 1
        print(f"{rounds:6d}  {honest_ok / trials:14.6f}  {fake_ok / trials:14.6f}")


def main():
    # A 5-cycle is 3-colourable.
    edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 4), (4, 0)]
    honest_coloring = {0: 'red', 1: 'green', 2: 'blue', 3: 'red', 4: 'green'}
    # Fake colouring has one bad edge: (4,0) both red.
    fake_coloring = {0: 'red', 1: 'green', 2: 'blue', 3: 'red', 4: 'red'}
    experiment(edges, honest_coloring, fake_coloring)
    print('\nInterpretation: a bad colouring may pass one challenge if the verifier misses the bad edge, but repeated random checks drive the cheating probability down.')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

10. Experiment 4: Schnorr identification: the algebraic sigma protocol Back to top

Experiment 4 Level 4 · modular groups and exponents Discrete logarithms

Question

Can the prover demonstrate knowledge of a secret exponent $x$ satisfying $y=g^x$ without revealing $x$?

Intuition

Schnorr identification is the central algebraic example in this laboratory. The public key is $y=g^x$. The prover hides fresh randomness $r$ inside a commitment, receives a random challenge $c$, and combines $r$, $c$, and the secret $x$ into a response.

Mathematical core

Work in a subgroup of order $q$ modulo prime $p$. The prover sends $$t=g^r\pmod p,$$ receives $c\in\mathbb Z_q$, and responds $$s=r+cx\pmod q.$$ The verifier checks $$g^s\stackrel{?}{\equiv}t\,y^c\pmod p.$$ Completeness follows because $$g^s=g^{r+cx}=g^r(g^x)^c=t\,y^c.$$

Concrete walkthrough

The script uses tiny values $p=23$, $q=11$, and $g=2$ so every number can be inspected. These values are deliberately insecure. In the sample transcript, the program prints the commitment, challenge, response, and both sides of the verification equation.

What you should learn

  • A sigma protocol has three moves: commitment, challenge, response.
  • Fresh randomness protects the secret while still allowing an algebraic check.
  • The verification equation works because exponents add when powers are multiplied.

Experiments to try

  • Print the hidden $x$ temporarily and verify $y=g^x\bmod p$ by hand.
  • Change one response value and observe verification fail.
  • Choose a challenge yourself and trace every modular calculation.
Self-check questions.
  1. Where does the secret $x$ appear in the public transcript?
  2. Why must the prover use a fresh $r$ for every execution?
  3. Which algebraic identity proves completeness?

04_schnorr_identification.py
Interactive Schnorr identification protocol.
Educational parameters only.

import random

q = 11
g = 2

    t = pow(g, r, p)

11. Experiment 5: Special soundness: extract the secret from two challenges Back to top

Experiment 5 Level 5 · proof of knowledge Witness extraction

Question

Why does answering two different challenges for the same Schnorr commitment demonstrate actual knowledge?

Intuition

A protocol can be convincing for more than an informal reason. In Schnorr, two accepting responses to different challenges but the same first message mathematically determine the witness.

Mathematical core

Suppose the same commitment $t=g^r$ appears in two accepting transcripts: $$s_1=r+c_1x\pmod q,\qquad s_2=r+c_2x\pmod q,$$ with $c_1\ne c_2$. Subtraction removes the hidden randomness: $$s_1-s_2=(c_1-c_2)x\pmod q.$$ Therefore $$x=(s_1-s_2)(c_1-c_2)^{-1}\pmod q.$$ This property is called special soundness.

Concrete walkthrough

The supplied output uses $q=11$, transcripts $(c_1,s_1)=(0,1)$ and $(c_2,s_2)=(1,9)$, and extracts $x=8$. Check it manually: $(1-9)(0-1)^{-1}\equiv(-8)(-1)^{-1}\equiv8\pmod{11}$.

What you should learn

  • Proof of knowledge can be formalized by an extractor, not just by confidence.
  • Reusing the same Schnorr randomness is catastrophic because it exposes the secret.
  • The challenge must be unpredictable when the commitment is chosen.

Experiments to try

  • Generate many pairs of challenges and confirm that extraction always recovers $x$.
  • Set $c_1=c_2$ and explain why the modular inverse no longer exists.
  • Modify one response so that it is not accepting; observe that extraction loses its guarantee.
Self-check questions.
  1. Why does subtracting the equations eliminate $r$?
  2. What real-world mistake is modeled by commitment or nonce reuse?
  3. Why is one transcript insufficient for this extractor?

05_schnorr_special_soundness.py
If the same Schnorr commitment answers two different challenges,
we can extract the secret x.
"""

from __future__ import annotations
import random

RNG = random.Random(1234)
p = 23
q = 11
g = 2


def main():
    x = RNG.randrange(1, q)
    y = pow(g, x, p)
    r = RNG.randrange(q)
    t = pow(g, r, p)

    c1, c2 = RNG.sample(range(q), 2)
    s1 = (r + c1 * x) % q
    s2 = (r + c2 * x) % q

    numerator = (s1 - s2) % q
    denominator = (c1 - c2) % q
    x_extracted = (numerator * pow(denominator, -1, q)) % q

    print('  s1 = r + c1 x (mod q)')
    print('  s2 = r + c2 x (mod q)')
    print('Subtract them:')
    print('  s1 - s2 = (c1 - c2) x (mod q)')
    print('So:')
    print('  x = (s1 - s2) / (c1 - c2) (mod q)')
    print()
    print(f'p={p}, q={q}, g={g}, y={y}')
    print(f'Hidden x = {x}')
    print(f'Common commitment t = {t}')
    print(f'Transcript 1: c1={c1}, s1={s1}')
    print(f'Transcript 2: c2={c2}, s2={s2}')
    print(f'Extracted x = {x_extracted}')
    print('Extraction successful?', x_extracted == x)


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

12. Experiment 6: Fiat–Shamir: make the proof non-interactive Back to top

Experiment 6 Level 6 · hashes as public challenges Non-interactive proof

Question

Can a hash function replace the live verifier’s random challenge?

Intuition

The Fiat–Shamir transform computes the challenge from the public statement, commitment, and message. Anyone can recompute the same challenge later, so a separate challenge message is no longer necessary.

Mathematical core

The prover first computes $t=g^r$. Instead of waiting for the verifier, compute $$c=H(\text{domain}\,\Vert\,y\,\Vert\,t\,\Vert\,m)\bmod q,$$ then output $(t,s)$ with $$s=r+cx\pmod q.$$ Verification recomputes $c$ and checks $g^s=t y^c$. Binding the message $m$ into the hash prevents the proof from being copied to a different statement.

Concrete walkthrough

The script proves the sentence “I know the secret key for this toy example.” It then changes the sentence while keeping the same proof values. Because the hash-derived challenge changes, the tampered proof fails.

What you should learn

  • Interaction can often be removed by deriving challenges from a cryptographic hash.
  • The statement and context must be included in the hash input.
  • Security analysis is normally expressed in the random-oracle model or related frameworks.

Experiments to try

  • Change one character in the message and inspect the new challenge.
  • Add an explicit domain-separation string such as `ZK-LAB-SCHNORR-V1`.
  • Serialize values in a fixed binary format instead of using a casual text separator.
Self-check questions.
  1. Why must the commitment be included in the hash?
  2. Why should two different protocols use different domain-separation tags?
  3. What part of the original three-move protocol has disappeared?

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
06_fiat_shamir_schnorr.py
Turn Schnorr's interactive protocol into a non-interactive proof
using the Fiat-Shamir transform.
"""

from __future__ import annotations
import hashlib
import random

RNG = random.Random(5)
p = 23
q = 11
g = 2


def hash_to_challenge(y: int, t: int, message: str) -> int:
    blob = f'{y}|{t}|{message}'.encode()
    return int(hashlib.sha256(blob).hexdigest(), 16) % q


def prove(x: int, y: int, message: str):
    r = RNG.randrange(q)
    t = pow(g, r, p)
    c = hash_to_challenge(y, t, message)
    s = (r + c * x) % q
    return {'t': t, 's': s, 'message': message}


def verify(y: int, proof: dict) -> bool:
    t, s, message = proof['t'], proof['s'], proof['message']
    c = hash_to_challenge(y, t, message)
    return pow(g, s, p) == (t * pow(y, c, p)) % p


def main():
    x = RNG.randrange(1, q)
    y = pow(g, x, p)
    message = 'I know the secret key for this toy example.'
    proof = prove(x, y, message)

    print('FIAT-SHAMIR NON-INTERACTIVE SCHNORR PROOF')
    print('=' * 60)
    print('Challenge is now computed, not sent by a live verifier:')
    print('  c = H(y || t || message) mod q')
    print('This removes interaction, at least in the random-oracle model.')
    print()
    print('Proof object:', proof)
    print('Verifier accepts original proof?', verify(y, proof))

    fake = dict(proof)
    fake['message'] = 'I changed the statement!'
    print('Verifier accepts tampered-message proof?', verify(y, fake))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

14. Experiment 8: OR composition: know one secret, hide which one Back to top

Experiment 8 Level 8 · simulated branches Partial knowledge

Question

Can the prover show knowledge of one of two secret exponents without revealing which secret is known?

Intuition

The prover constructs one genuine Schnorr branch and one simulated branch. The verifier sees two valid-looking transcripts whose challenge pieces add to one global challenge. Because either branch could have been the simulated one, the transcript hides the witness location.

Mathematical core

For branches $i=1,2$, verification requires $$g^{s_i}=a_i y_i^{c_i}.$$ The global challenge is divided so that $$c_1+c_2\equiv c\pmod q.$$ On the unknown branch, choose $c_i,s_i$ first and solve backward: $$a_i=g^{s_i}y_i^{-c_i}.$$ On the known branch, choose a genuine commitment and set its challenge piece after the global $c$ is known.

Concrete walkthrough

The key conceptual leap is that an accepting-looking transcript can be created without a witness when the challenge is chosen in advance. That is not cheating here; it is deliberately used inside a larger protocol so that the verifier cannot distinguish the real branch from the simulated branch.

What you should learn

  • Simulation can be used as a construction technique, not only in a security proof.
  • Challenge splitting links all branches to one unpredictable global challenge.
  • The result is witness-indistinguishable: the verifier cannot tell which witness was used.

Experiments to try

  • Switch which branch is genuine while keeping the output format unchanged.
  • Tamper with one challenge piece so the sum condition fails.
  • Extend the code to three branches before reading Experiment 10.
Self-check questions.
  1. How can the simulated branch verify without knowing its secret?
  2. Why is the global challenge not chosen until after both commitments exist?
  3. What information about the known branch is absent from the transcript?

"""
08_or_proof_one_of_two.py
A sigma-protocol OR proof: the prover knows one of two secrets,
but does not reveal which one.
"""

from __future__ import annotations
import random

RNG = random.Random(2026)
p = 23
q = 11
g = 2


def inv_mod(a: int, mod: int) -> int:
    return pow(a, -1, mod)


def main():
    # Prover really knows x1 but not x2.
    x1 = RNG.randrange(1, q)
    x2 = RNG.randrange(1, q)
    y1 = pow(g, x1, p)
    y2 = pow(g, x2, p)

    # Prover prepares one real branch (for y1) and one simulated branch (for y2).
    r1 = RNG.randrange(q)
    a1 = pow(g, r1, p)

    c2 = RNG.randrange(q)
    s2 = RNG.randrange(q)
    a2 = (pow(g, s2, p) * inv_mod(pow(y2, c2, p), p)) % p

    c = RNG.randrange(q)  # verifier's global challenge
    c1 = (c - c2) % q
    s1 = (r1 + c1 * x1) % q

    check_sum = (c1 + c2) % q == c
    check1 = pow(g, s1, p) == (a1 * pow(y1, c1, p)) % p
    check2 = pow(g, s2, p) == (a2 * pow(y2, c2, p)) % p

    print('OR PROOF: KNOW ONE OF TWO DISCRETE LOGS')
    print('=' * 60)
    print('Global challenge is split as c = c1 + c2 (mod q).')
    print('One branch is real, the other branch is simulated.')
    print('The verifier cannot tell which is which.')
    print()
    print(f'y1 = {y1}, y2 = {y2}')
    print(f'Global challenge c = {c}')
    print(f'Branch 1: a1={a1}, c1={c1}, s1={s1}')
    print(f'Branch 2: a2={a2}, c2={c2}, s2={s2}')
    print()
    print('Checks:')
    print('  c1 + c2 == c (mod q)?', check_sum)
    print('  Branch 1 equation holds?', check1)
    print('  Branch 2 equation holds?', check2)
    print('Overall accept?', check_sum and check1 and check2)


if __name__ == '__main__':

Experiment 9 Level 9 · parallel composition Multiple witnesses

Question

How can simple proofs be combined to prove that two separate witnesses are both known?

Intuition

Run two Schnorr proofs in parallel under the same verifier challenge. The verifier accepts only if both equations pass. This is a clean example of building a larger logical statement from smaller protocols.

For public values $y_1=g^{x_1}$ and $y_2=g^{x_2}$, choose $r_1,r_2$ and send $$a_1=g^{r_1},\qquad a_2=g^{r_2}.$$ After one shared challenge $c$, respond $$s_1=r_1+cx_1,\qquad s_2=r_2+cx_2\pmod q.$$ Verify both $$g^{s_1}=a_1y_1^c,\qquad g^{s_2}=a_2y_2^c.$$

The logical structure is “statement 1 AND statement 2.” A single failed branch rejects the complete proof. Sharing the challenge keeps the executions tied to the same verifier randomness.

  • Proof systems can be composed according to logical connectives.
  • Parallel composition lets one challenge test multiple witness relations.
  • The verifier’s rule mirrors Boolean AND: every branch must pass.

16. Experiment 10: Many-way OR proof: ring-style anonymous membership Back to top

Experiment 10 Level 10 · anonymity sets and scaling One witness among many

Question

Can the prover show that one secret key belongs to a public list without identifying which public key is theirs?

Intuition

This is the many-branch extension of the OR proof. All branches except one are simulated. The one genuine branch absorbs whatever challenge value is needed so that all challenge pieces add to the verifier’s global challenge.

Mathematical core

For public keys $y_1,\ldots,y_n$, each branch must satisfy $$g^{s_i}=a_i y_i^{c_i}.$$ All challenge pieces obey $$\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_i\equiv c\pmod q.$$ For simulated branches, select $(c_i,s_i)$ and compute $a_i=g^{s_i}y_i^{-c_i}$. For the known branch $k$, choose real randomness $r_k$, set $$c_k=c-\sum_{i\ne k}c_i\pmod q,$$ and answer $s_k=r_k+c_kx_k$.

Concrete walkthrough

The provided script uses five public keys and secretly knows the key at index 3. Every branch verifies, and the challenge pieces sum correctly. The final transcript contains no explicit “known index” field; the printed index is included only as an educational comment after verification.

What you should learn

  • An anonymity set can be modeled as a many-way OR statement.
  • Proof size in this straightforward construction grows linearly with the number of branches.
  • The verifier learns membership in the set, not the identity of the member.

Experiments to try

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
10_ring_style_membership_proof.py
Generalized OR proof across many public keys.
This gives a toy ring-proof / anonymous-membership flavour.
"""

from __future__ import annotations
import random

RNG = random.Random(2718)
p = 23
q = 11
g = 2


def inv_mod(a: int, mod: int) -> int:
    return pow(a, -1, mod)


def main():
    n = 5
    secrets = RNG.sample(range(1, q), n)
    public_keys = [pow(g, x, p) for x in secrets]

    known_index = 3  # prover knows only this secret
    xk = secrets[known_index]

    c_values = [None] * n
    s_values = [None] * n
    a_values = [None] * n

    # Simulate all branches except the known one.
    for i in range(n):
        if i == known_index:
            continue
        c_values[i] = RNG.randrange(q)
        s_values[i] = RNG.randrange(q)
        a_values[i] = (pow(g, s_values[i], p) * inv_mod(pow(public_keys[i], c_values[i], p), p)) % p

    # Real branch
    rk = RNG.randrange(q)
    a_values[known_index] = pow(g, rk, p)

    # Global challenge from verifier
    c_global = RNG.randrange(q)
    known_sum = sum(c for c in c_values if c is not None) % q
    c_values[known_index] = (c_global - known_sum) % q
    s_values[known_index] = (rk + c_values[known_index] * xk) % q

    # Verify
    challenge_sum_ok = sum(c_values) % q == c_global
    branch_checks = []
    for i in range(n):
        lhs = pow(g, s_values[i], p)
        rhs = (a_values[i] * pow(public_keys[i], c_values[i], p)) % p
        branch_checks.append(lhs == rhs)

    print('RING-STYLE MEMBERSHIP / MANY-WAY OR PROOF')
    print('=' * 60)
    print('Interpretation: the prover shows, "I know one secret key in this public list,')
    print('but I will not reveal which one."')
    print()
    print('Public keys:', public_keys)
    print('Global challenge:', c_global)
    print('Challenge pieces c_i:', c_values)
    print('Response pieces  s_i:', s_values)
    print('Commitments      a_i:', a_values)
    print()
    print('Do the challenge pieces sum to the global challenge?', challenge_sum_ok)
    for i, ok in enumerate(branch_checks):
        print(f'Branch {i} verifies? {ok}')
    print('Overall accept?', challenge_sum_ok and all(branch_checks))
    print(f'(The prover actually knew index {known_index}, but the transcript does not announce that.)')


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()

17. From sigma protocols to modern zero-knowledge systems Back to top

Experiments 4–10 prove relations involving a few modular-exponent equations. Modern systems aim to prove that a much larger computation was executed correctly. The central strategy is to translate the computation into algebraic constraints, commit to a hidden execution trace, and prove that the committed values satisfy those constraints.

Important simplification. “SNARK” and “STARK” name broad families, not single fixed algorithms. Concrete systems differ in arithmetization, polynomial commitments, setup, proof size, prover cost, verifier cost, recursion, and security assumptions.

18. Worked bridge example: a private arithmetic circuit Back to top

Suppose the public claim is: “I know a private integer $x$ such that $x^3+x+5=35$.” The witness is $x=3$, but the verifier should learn only that some valid witness exists.

$$a=x\cdot x,\qquad b=a\cdot x,\qquad c=b+x,\qquad c+5=35.$$
Wire or gateConstraintValue for $x=3$
Input$x$ is private3
Multiplication gate 1$a=x^2$9
Multiplication gate 2$b=ax=x^3$27
Addition gate$c=b+x$30
Public output check$c+5=35$True

A modern proof system does not usually send all these private wire values directly. Instead, it encodes the trace into algebraic objects and proves consistency with the constraints. This is the large-scale descendant of the same theme already visible in Schnorr: hide witness-dependent values inside randomized algebra while preserving checkable equations.

19. zk-SNARKs and zk-STARKs Back to top

The names describe different design goals. A zk-SNARK is a zero-knowledge succinct non-interactive argument of knowledge. A zk-STARK is a zero-knowledge scalable transparent argument of knowledge. The comparison below describes common tendencies, not universal laws.

FeatureCommon pairing-based zk-SNARK patternCommon hash-based zk-STARK pattern
InteractionNon-interactive proof objectNon-interactive after Fiat–Shamir transforms an interactive-oracle-style protocol
SetupMany systems use a structured reference string; some modern SNARKs use universal, updatable, or transparent setupsDesigned to be transparent, avoiding a secret trusted setup
Proof sizeOften extremely small in pairing-based constructionsTypically larger because low-degree testing and query openings are included
Main assumptionsOften elliptic-curve pairings plus polynomial-commitment assumptionsTypically cryptographic hashes plus information-theoretic low-degree testing
Quantum outlookCommon pairing-based forms are not considered post-quantumHash-based designs are commonly presented as post-quantum candidates
Best choice?Application-dependent: proof size, prover resources, verification environment, setup policy, recursion, and security model all matter.
Connection to this laboratory. Schnorr supplies the commit–challenge–response pattern; Fiat–Shamir removes interaction; commitments hide large objects; special soundness motivates proof-of-knowledge extraction; and composition shows how small relations can be assembled into richer statements.

20. Common misunderstandings Back to top

MisunderstandingCorrection
“Zero knowledge means the verifier learns nothing.”The verifier learns the validity of the public statement, but should learn no extra witness information.
“A hash alone creates a zero-knowledge proof.”Hashes may implement commitments or Fiat–Shamir challenges, but the protocol structure and security proof are essential.
“Small modular examples are secure because the equations are correct.”Correct algebra is not enough. Security requires large parameters, safe groups, precise encodings, robust randomness, and vetted implementations.
“A simulated transcript means anyone can impersonate the prover.”The simulator controls or predicts the challenge in the security experiment. A live cheater must commit before seeing an unpredictable challenge.
“All SNARKs need trusted setup; all STARKs are always faster.”Both are families with many designs. Setup and performance claims must be attached to a specific construction and parameter set.
“Zero knowledge automatically hides metadata.”The proof may hide a witness while network timing, public inputs, proof size, or surrounding application data still reveal information.
