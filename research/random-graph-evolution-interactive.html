Interactive Random Graph Evolution Laboratory

Choose the number of vertices and the final number of edges, then watch a simple graph grow one randomly selected edge at a time. The page continuously identifies connected components, colors the current largest component blue, records structural milestones, and produces a detailed report when the target graph is complete.

The process samples edges without replacement from all n(n-1)/2 possible undirected edges. Consequently, the final graph is a uniform sample from the classical fixed-edge random graph model G(n,m). A seed makes the entire experiment reproducible.

Color rule. Blue always denotes the current largest connected component. Gold denotes the newest edge. Smaller nontrivial components receive separate muted colors, while isolated vertices remain grey. Here “giant component” is used visually for the dominant component; the final commentary distinguishes this finite observation from the formal asymptotic meaning.

3. What the animation is measuring

Connected components

After each edge is added, a graph search partitions the vertices into connected components. The largest is colored blue. When two components merge, all vertices in the newly dominant component immediately inherit the blue encoding.

Cycle rank

The live cycle statistic is m - n + c, where c is the number of connected components. It counts the number of independent cycles: zero means the graph is a forest.

Final diagnostics

At completion, the page computes component sizes, degree summaries, triangles, mean local clustering, bipartiteness, bridges, articulation vertices, and a diameter for the whole graph or its largest component.

Randomness and reproducibility

All possible edges are shuffled by a deterministic seeded generator. Resetting preserves the edge order; choosing a new seed produces another graph. No server, library, or stored user data is used.

4. Interpreting the familiar random-graph thresholds

In the classical Erdős–Rényi process, the scale m ≈ n/2 is associated with the emergence of a component containing a positive fraction of all vertices. Connectivity occurs later, around m ≈ (n log n)/2. These are asymptotic guideposts rather than guarantees for a particular finite sample, which is why the live experiment is useful: individual seeded realizations can cross the same qualitative transitions earlier or later.

Practical limit: the page permits up to 150 vertices. Dense graphs can contain thousands of SVG edges, so force-directed motion is progressively reduced as density rises. The filled-circle and circular-ring layouts are useful for large or dense examples because they keep vertex positions stable.
