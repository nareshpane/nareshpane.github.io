The broader policy lens

Budget Impact Analysis in Broad Context

What changes in a budget when a policy, service or technology is introduced? Budget impact analysis (BIA) examines that financial question over a defined time horizon. It is common in health technology assessment and public drug-plan decisions; related financial-impact questions arise across the policy areas below.

Related questions: Cost-benefit analysis weighs benefits and costs in monetary terms; cost-effectiveness analysis relates costs to outcomes. Affordability analysis asks whether resources suffice, while fiscal planning places spending and revenues in a wider budget. Methods and terminology vary by program, jurisdiction and decision.

Orientation: ISPOR BIA good-practice guidance; HM Treasury Green Book: appraisal and financial impact.

This British Columbia (BC) case study concerns hidradenitis suppurativa (HS). Monetary figures are in Canadian dollars (CAD); CAD $000 denotes thousands of dollars.

The answer depends on eligible patients, existing medication use, dosing schedules, prices, reimbursement rules, and how many patients switch treatments.

01 / Define the indication before counting people

A condition, a treatment pathway, an eligible subset

For this study, we rely on publicly available information and carefully documented clinical and economic assumptions. Our main reference is a pharmacoeconomic review published by Canada’s Drug Agency / L’Agence des médicaments du Canada (CDA-AMC), formerly the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH). We focus on hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), a chronic inflammatory skin condition, and secukinumab, marketed as Cosentyx. Review, pp. 9–12, 143–148.

We illustrate how an economist could construct a BC budget impact analysis; we do not possess actual provincial patient-level records. The reviewed indication is adults with moderate-to-severe HS who responded inadequately to conventional systemic therapy. A diagnosis alone does not establish biologic eligibility or public reimbursement.

Clinical severity: a physician’s assessment

Hurley stage and inadequate treatment response usually cannot be established from diagnostic codes alone. Clinical documentation refines the estimated cohort. Current BC initial targeted-treatment criteria require Hurley stage II or III. HLTH 5485, July 24, 2025, p. 2.

Who approves a drug, and who pays for it?

From Health Canada’s authorization to BC PharmaCare reimbursement

A simplified pathway: evidence reviews, negotiations and provincial assessment can overlap. Authorization or a positive recommendation does not guarantee reimbursement.

Research and marketing authorization

Manufacturers develop medicines and sponsor clinical research. Health Canada regulates trials conducted in Canada under applicable requirements; it does not conduct every trial. It reviews safety, efficacy and quality evidence for marketing authorization. A Notice of Compliance (NOC) follows a satisfactory submission review. Clinical-trial guidance; Health Canada Notice of Compliance explanation.

Evidence, advice and negotiation

Canada’s Drug Agency (CDA-AMC) assesses comparative clinical and economic evidence, including cost-effectiveness, budget impact, uncertainty, and patient and clinician perspectives. Its reimbursement recommendations advise public plans; they do not automatically require BC coverage. Where applicable, the pan-Canadian Pharmaceutical Alliance (pCPA) negotiates pricing arrangements, whose net prices are generally confidential. CDA-AMC reviews; recommendation and next-step guidance, p. 9; pCPA process.

Provincial implementation

BC makes coverage and implementation decisions through its drug-review and PharmaCare processes. Authorization to sell a medication is different from public reimbursement. Plan eligibility, indication, clinical requirements and pricing policy determine applicable coverage. BC drug-review decisions; Special Authority policy.

Appraise the assumptions

Population size, market shares, uptake, comparator costs, adherence, discontinuation and clinical effects require scrutiny. Sponsor and payer analyses may differ in perspective and interpretation. The historical pharmacoeconomic review reported two three-year incremental budget impact estimates for participating Canadian public drug plans: CAD $76.54 million in the sponsor’s submission and CAD $9.55 million in the CDA-AMC reanalysis. Revised eligible-population, biologic-use, coverage and switching assumptions, together with methodological corrections, materially changed the forecast. Neither aggregate is British Columbia’s actual expenditure or a complete BC-specific assessment of negotiated net prices and reimbursement arrangements. Inspect the actual revisions; Review, pp. 173–179.

Clinical eligibility → coverage review → plan payment

Special Authority: clinical eligibility is not the same as financial coverage

In British Columbia, Special Authority (SA) is a PharmaCare process for coverage of certain medicines and indications against established criteria. A prescriber generally submits clinical documentation for review; approval allows coverage under the patient’s applicable plan and reimbursement rules and does not necessarily mean the patient pays nothing. Special Authority overview.

The current HLTH 5485: Targeted Therapies for Hidradenitis Suppurativa is dated July 24, 2025 (checked October 20, 2026). Its initial request combines adult age, active moderate-to-severe HS, Hurley II/III, an inadequate response to a recent continuous 90-day optimally dosed oral-antibiotic trial, and specified lesion criteria. A dermatologist documents the findings and body map.

Fictional teaching example · not an official determination

Initial requests require ≥3 abscesses/nodules, ≥2 anatomical areas and ≥1 draining sinus/fistula. Switching uses a separate six-month request documenting the prior biologic and response; renewal uses a one-year request documenting maintained response. Only one biologic is covered at a time. Form, pp. 1–2.

Approval ≠ free medication. Physicians and PharmaCare make actual clinical and coverage decisions under current policies. Approved coverage remains subject to the person’s PharmaCare plan and applicable pricing rules; explore payment separately.

The first estimation challenge

How many residents have the indication?

Our first challenge is estimating how many BC residents have the indication. One approach applies a published prevalence rate to a compatible population. Another identifies potential cases directly from linked administrative health records.

A · Published prevalence estimate
Nestimated = (r / 100,000) × P

r = prevalence per 100,000; P = population for the same age universe. Both values below are hypothetical, not current BC observations.

B · Administrative case ascertainment

Merge candidate Discharge Abstract Database (DAD), Medical Services Plan (MSP) and National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS) records, link patient keys, remove duplicate encounters and validate the definition. Physician care can identify a potential case without a hospital admission, so hospital-only counting may undercount people.

Laboratory data can help identify cohorts for indications with validated laboratory-based case definitions. For hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), the Provincial Laboratory Information Solution (PLIS) provides supplementary context, not a stand-alone diagnostic source; access remains conditional. Administrative capture depends on diagnosis, care use, coding and the observation window. Explore the linked synthetic cohort.

Condition prevalence ≠ reimbursement eligibility. Clinical criteria, treatment history, actual utilization and plan eligibility narrow the population further.

Published prevalence may include undiagnosed people. Administrative ascertainment may miss them or include miscoded cases. Different observation windows and healthcare access also produce differences. The two approaches are not forced to agree; neither replaces the existing historical adult-cohort model.

04 / A separate top-down approximation

From 4.48 million adults to a small treated cohort

Prevalence, diagnosis, severity, biologic eligibility, actual use and public reimbursement describe different populations. This historical model uses biologic utilization as a proxy for the treated market; it does not count every clinically eligible untreated adult.

Conditional probabilities, capture and validation

Each fraction must refer to the population surviving the preceding stage. Multiplication does not require statistical independence when these are conditional probabilities; multiplying unrelated marginal estimates can be misleading. The report's 3.5% uptake was back-calculated, rather than directly measured from linked BC records.

A validation-based correction might use Ntrue ≈ Nrecorded × positive predictive value (PPV) / sensitivity, provided the validation sample transports to the study population. This is not an automatic correction for missing data.

Capture–recapture is not applied. It would require defensible assumptions about source dependence, a closed population, linkage accuracy and heterogeneous capture probabilities; care-seeking links these databases strongly.

Unit-cost arithmetic and exact annual calculation

Cost = Σ events [units × unit price × (1 − discount) × (1 + acquisition growth)forecast year−1 × (1 + markup) + dispensing fee + administration cost]. Fees are per administration as an explicit simplification. When the base interval is q2w, everyone already receives that regimen and the escalation fraction has no additional effect. The escalation fraction mixes complete q4w and q2w courses; it is not an estimated clinical escalation rate.

For established patients, continue the original event schedule across annual boundaries. Patients switching later in a year incur their prior adalimumab events only before the switch and secukinumab events thereafter. No dose is charged twice at a boundary. Dosing units can be fractional only when averaging expected patients or regimen mixtures.

12 / Methodology and practical implementation

Build an auditable cohort and an auditable counterfactual

Case ascertainment

Specify index date, lookback, residency, adult age and a common observation window. Define point prevalence at a date or period prevalence over an interval; incident cases require an adequate prior washout. Verify coding versions, diagnostic positions and encounter completeness before applying candidate rules.

Repeated encounters measure care use, not new people. Link across systems, deduplicate encounters, then count unique patients. Missing diagnoses, delayed diagnosis and misclassification can bias both numerator and eligibility. NACRS non-capture does not prove no emergency care. Codes rarely establish Hurley stage or inadequate response. Validated laboratory-based definitions may identify cohorts for other indications; for HS, PLIS remains supplementary and access-dependent.

Population estimation

Top-down approximations and bottom-up records estimate different constructs. Access to care, diagnosis capture, enrollment, indication confirmation and private treatment can all explain disagreement. Validate against sampled charts or registries, report sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV) with transportability limitations, and retain alternative definitions.

Growth in biologic utilization is distinct from population growth or incidence. Do not apply multiple growth factors to the same incoming patients. Keep actively treated patients distinct from historical discontinuers and untreated eligible patients.

Economic interpretation

The public perspective includes public costs; patient burden and private payments need separate information. List prices differ from confidential net acquisition costs. State price date, unit, pack size, fees, markups and treatment duration. No rebates are inferred here.

Induction belongs to new treatment courses, not every established patient every year. Include nonadherence, escalation and discontinuation in transparent scenarios. Annual impact is a flow; cumulative impact is the sum. Round only displayed outputs and identify expected counts versus discrete synthetic people.

Affordability versus value

A BIA asks how much a program may spend over a specified horizon under alternative treatment markets. Cost-effectiveness analysis also considers health outcomes relative to costs, often using quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) and a longer horizon. A favourable BIA does not establish value or comparative effectiveness.

This counterfactual uses only adalimumab biosimilars as the reference biologic. It excludes broader current-market changes, clinical outcomes, adverse-event costs, background treatment, wound care and service offsets. It is a teaching model, not a decision-ready submission.

Limitations and Sources of Uncertainty

Prevalence uncertainty

Published estimates may not transfer to BC because age structure, care use and diagnosis capture differ. A different denominator or rate changes the eligible-cohort scale.

Administrative identification

False positives, false negatives, repeated encounters and missing records affect case counts. Deduplication removes repeated people but does not eliminate diagnostic misclassification. Laboratory-based case definitions can support other indications; PLIS does not independently diagnose HS and its availability must be confirmed.

Clinical eligibility

Severity and inadequate prior response are rarely fully observable in billing or discharge codes. Chart review, registries or validated proxies may materially change the eligible subset.

Treatment switching

The 30%, 40% and 50% default cumulative shares are hypothetical. Different uptake changes treatment stocks, displaced comparator use and the forecast.

Drug prices

Public benchmark prices may differ from confidential government net prices. Biosimilar prices, negotiated arrangements, fees and markups can change the incremental impact.

Dosing and adherence

Escalation, delayed injections, missed doses and discontinuation change realized use. Recommended schedules alone do not establish actual acquisition expenditure.

Clinical effectiveness

Comparative efficacy and tolerability remain uncertain. Response and discontinuation affect utilization; no secukinumab superiority over adalimumab is assumed.

Public coverage and patient burden

Plan eligibility, Special Authority, deductibles, private coverage and pricing rules affect payment. A public BIA does not represent the patient’s complete financial burden.

Change the assumptions, change the budget

These model-derived scenarios vary the current cohort and switching shares together. They are illustrative contrasts, not confidence intervals or a ranking guaranteed under every price configuration.

The estimated budget impact is conditional on the patient population, treatment behaviour, medication prices, and reimbursement rules assumed in the analysis.
